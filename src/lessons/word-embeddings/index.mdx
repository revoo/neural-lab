# Word Embeddings

At the end of <LessonLink to="one-hot-vs-dense">the one-hot vs dense lesson</LessonLink>, we left a cliff-hanger: if dense semantic vectors beat one-hot vectors so decisively, **where do the numbers come from?** Nobody is going to hand-label 300 coordinates for every word in the English language!

The idea that a word's meaning comes from its neighbours goes back to linguistics in the 1950s — Zellig Harris (1954) and J. R. Firth (1957), who popularised the idea that "a word is characterized by the company it keeps". <Src href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributional_semantics" />

That is the **<Term id="distributional-hypothesis">distributional hypothesis</Term>**. Even if you have never read a dictionary definition of *king* and *queen*, notice that both appear beside *royal*, *crown*, and *palace* — while *king* co-occurs with *male* and *man*, and *queen* co-occurs with *female* and *woman*.

In 2013, Tomáš Mikolov and colleagues at Google turned that insight into **<Term id="word2vec">Word2Vec</Term>**, a fast neural method that learns dense word vectors from surrounding context in unlabelled text — no human labels required, just text. <Src href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word2Vec" />

## 1. Skip-Gram with Negative Sampling (SGNS)

Given a centre word $\ca{c}$ (say `"king"`) and a real neighbouring context word $\cg{o}$ inside window $\pm W$ (say `"royal"`), we want their dot product $\cb{u_o^\top v_c}$ to be **large and positive**.

Why not just put a full softmax over the entire vocabulary of $V = 100,000$ words? Because computing the softmax denominator requires **100,000 dot products for every single word pair** — impossibly slow on billions of tokens!

**<Term id="negative-sampling">Negative sampling</Term>** turns a 100,000-way softmax into $1 + K$ tiny binary logistic regressions: pull the 1 real neighbour $\cg{o}$ *together*, and push $K$ randomly drawn noise words $\cc{n_1, \dots, n_K}$ *apart*:

<Eq
  words="SGNS loss  =  reward real neighbour  +  push K random noise words away"
  tex="\mathcal{L} = -\log \sigma(\cb{u_o^\top} \ca{v_c}) - \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log \sigma(-\cc{u_{n_k}^\top} \ca{v_c})"
>
  - $\ca{v_c} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ — centre-word embedding for word $c$
  - $\cb{u_o} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ — context embedding for the real neighbouring word $o$
  - $\cc{u_{n_k}} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ — context embeddings of $K$ random noise words (typically $K = 5$, drawn from unigram frequency$^{0.75}$)
  - $\sigma(z) = 1 / (1 + e^{-z})$ — sigmoid probability (wants $\cb{u_o^\top}\ca{v_c} \gg 0$ and $\cc{u_{n_k}^\top}\ca{v_c} \ll 0$)
</Eq>

With $K = 5$ on a 100,000-word vocabulary, SGNS computes **6** dot products per pair instead of **100,000** — a **16,667× speedup**!

## 2. Linear analogies: `king − man + woman ≈ queen`

Why do linear vector analogies emerge out of a dumb co-occurrence game?

Because SGNS implicitly factorises the log co-occurrence ratio (pointwise mutual information, PMI — proven by Levy & Goldberg 2014). Moving from `"man"` to `"woman"` shifts co-occurrence log-odds along a consistent **gender direction** (`male → female`). Moving from `"king"` to `"queen"` shifts log-odds by the **exact same gender ratio** while keeping royalty fixed — so their embedding vectors form a geometric **parallelogram**!

<Callout kind="warn" title="Two honest caveats about Word2Vec analogies">
  First, standard analogy evaluation (including Mikolov et al. 2013) **explicitly excludes the three query words `a, b, c`** when searching for the nearest neighbour — otherwise `king − man + woman` often lands closer to `king` itself when the gender shift is smaller than the royalty distance! Second, static word embeddings assign **one fixed vector per word**, so `"river bank"` and `"savings bank"` get mashed into a single averaged vector. That polysemy bottleneck is exactly why Transformers (<ModuleRef id="transformers" short />) run self-attention on top of an embedding table to build *contextual* embeddings.
</Callout>

<ForReal>

Even after Transformers replaced static word vectors for NLP, **Skip-Gram with Negative Sampling (`gensim.models.Word2Vec`)** remains a workhorse everywhere discrete sequences appear without labels: Spotify, Airbnb, and e-commerce teams train "Item2Vec" embeddings over playlist, listing, and product-click sessions (`sg=1, negative=5, ns_exponent=0.75`) to power candidate generation in milliseconds. The exact same pull-positives-push-negatives idea also evolved directly into contrastive representation learning (CLIP, SimCLR) and in-batch negatives for modern dense retrievers.

## 4. One exact SGNS step in 10 lines of NumPy

```python
import numpy as np

def sigmoid(x): return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-x))

v_c = np.array([0.4, -0.2, 0.1])
u_pos = np.array([0.3, 0.5, -0.1])
u_negs = np.array([[-0.2, 0.4, 0.6], [0.5, -0.3, 0.2]])

p_pos = sigmoid(v_c @ u_pos)
p_negs = sigmoid(u_negs @ v_c)  # probability wrongly assigned to noise words
loss = -np.log(p_pos) - np.sum(np.log(1.0 - p_negs))
grad_vc = (p_pos - 1.0) * u_pos + np.sum(p_negs[:, None] * u_negs, axis=0)
print(f"SGNS loss: {loss:.4f} | grad_vc: {np.round(grad_vc, 4).tolist()}")
# SGNS loss: 2.1755 | grad_vc: [0.0405, -0.2296, 0.4487]
```
