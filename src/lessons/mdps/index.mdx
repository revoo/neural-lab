## The problem: no teacher, delayed feedback, and consequences

Everything you have built so far — classifiers, regressors, even language models — had a remarkably patient teacher. On every single example, the teacher handed you the exact right answer: *"this image is a 7"*, *"the next token was `the`"*. You compared your prediction with the target, computed a gradient, and adjusted the weights.

Now imagine teaching a robot to cross a warehouse, an algorithm to play Go, or a reasoning model to write a working program. Nobody hands you the optimal move at step 4. Instead:

1. **You only get a sparse, delayed score** — a win or a loss forty moves later, or a unit test suite that passes or fails at the end.
2. **Your own actions change the future.** Turn left instead of right and you enter a completely different room with different choices ahead.
3. **The world pushes back unpredictably.** A wheel slips on a wet tile; an opponent surprises you.

This is the world of **reinforcement learning** (RL): learning *what to do* from consequences rather than labels. And before an agent can learn from trial and error, we need a clean language for describing the game it is playing — and what it even means for a strategy to be "optimal".

<Analogy title="Think of a delivery robot on a battery budget">

Picture a small robot navigating a warehouse floor. Every square it can stand on is a **state**. The four buttons on its controller — up, right, down, left — are its **actions**. Each step drains a little battery (`−0.1`), falling down a loading pit wrecks a wheel (`−10`), a nearby charging coin pays `+2`, and reaching the main Vault pays `+10`.

Notice the two traps right away:
- **Short-term greed:** grabbing the nearby `+2` coin feels good immediately, but ends the trip and throws away the `+10` Vault five squares away.
- **Hidden risk:** walking straight along the edge of the loading pits is the shortest route, yet if the floor is even slightly slippery, one bad wheel slip wipes out the entire run.

</Analogy>

To reason about trade-offs like these mathematically, we package the robot's world into five pieces — a **<Term id="mdp">Markov Decision Process (MDP)</Term>**:

- **States** $s \in \mathcal{S}$: every situation the agent can find itself in (here, a grid square). "Markov" simply means the current state holds everything that matters for the future — you don't need the full history of how you got there.
- **Actions** $a \in \mathcal{A}$: the choices available at each state (`↑`, `→`, `↓`, `←`).
- **Transitions** $P(s' \mid s, a)$: the physics of the world — if you are in state $s$ and try action $a$, the probability of landing in $s'$.
- **Rewards** $R(s, a, s')$: the immediate score you collect on that step.
- **Discount factor** $\gamma \in [0, 1)$: how much you value a reward one step from now compared to right now.

A strategy — a rule telling the agent which arrow to press in every square — is called a **<Term id="policy">policy</Term>**, written $\pi(a \mid s)$.

## How patient should an agent be? The discount factor

Suppose an episode yields a sequence of rewards $R_1, R_2, R_3, \dots$. Why not simply add them all up? Two reasons: if an episode can run forever, an undiscounted sum can blow up to infinity; and in practice, a pound today is worth more than a pound ten steps from now when the world is uncertain.

So we multiply each future reward by a power of the **<Term id="discount-factor">discount factor</Term>** $\gamma$:

<Eq
  words="\text{total discounted return} = \text{reward now} + \cp{\gamma}\cdot\text{next reward} + \cp{\gamma}^2\cdot\text{two steps later} + \cdots"
  tex="\cc{G_t} = \cb{R_{t+1}} + \cp{\gamma}\,\cb{R_{t+2}} + \cp{\gamma}^2\,\cb{R_{t+3}} + \cdots = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \cp{\gamma}^k\,\cb{R_{t+k+1}}"
>

- $\cc{G_t}$ — the **return** starting from time step $t$: the total score the agent cares about maximising
- $\cb{R_{t+k+1}}$ — the immediate reward earned $k+1$ steps from now
- $\cp{\gamma}$ — the **discount factor** between $0$ and $1$: $\gamma = 0$ is totally myopic (only the very next step counts); $\gamma = 0.90$ gives an effective planning horizon of roughly $1/(1-\gamma) = 10$ steps

</Eq>

## Value functions and the Bellman equation

How did the widget find that exact optimal policy instantaneously? Through the central idea of reinforcement learning: the **<Term id="value-function">value function</Term>**.

Instead of enumerating every possible path to the end of time (which explodes exponentially with length), attach **one cached number** to every square:

- $V^\pi(s)$ is the expected discounted return if you start in square $s$ and follow policy $\pi$.
- $V^*(s)$ is the best possible expected return you could ever achieve from square $s$ under *any* policy.

Now ask a beautifully recursive question — Richard Bellman's 1957 insight: *if you already knew the true optimal values $V^*(s')$ of your neighbouring squares, how would you compute the optimal value of the square you're standing on right now?*

You would just look **one step ahead**. For each of your four actions, compute the expected immediate reward plus $\gamma$ times the cached value of wherever you land — and pick the action with the highest total:

<Eq
  words="\text{best value here} = \max_{\text{action}} \;\; \mathbb{E}\Big[\,\text{immediate reward} + \cp{\gamma}\cdot\text{best value next}\,\Big]"
  tex="\ca{V^*(s)} = \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \sum_{s'} \cp{P(s' \mid s, a)}\Big(\cb{R(s, a, s')} + \cp{\gamma}\,\ca{V^*(s')}\Big)"
>

- $\ca{V^*(s)}$ — the **optimal state value**: best expected return starting from state $s$
- $\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}}$ — try every available action and keep the highest expected score (its winner is the optimal arrow $\pi^*(s)$!)
- $\cp{P(s' \mid s, a)}$ — transition probability of landing in next state $s'$ when trying action $a$
- $\cb{R(s, a, s')}$ — immediate reward received on that single step
- $\cp{\gamma}\,\ca{V^*(s')}$ — discounted value of all future steps from $s'$ onward, already summarised in one number

</Eq>

That equation is the **<Term id="bellman-equation">Bellman optimality equation</Term>**. If you're a software engineer, you already recognise what it is: **dynamic programming**. Just like shortest-path algorithms cache the distance from each neighbour so you never recompute the whole tail of a path, Bellman's equation caches the entire future in $V^*(s')$.

Better yet, it gives us a dirt-simple algorithm called **<Term id="value-iteration">Value Iteration</Term>**:

1. Start with a terrible guess: $V_0(s) = 0$ for every square.
2. Sweep across every square and overwrite $V_{k+1}(s)$ using the right-hand side of Bellman's equation with the previous table $V_k$.
3. Repeat until the numbers stop changing.

Because $\gamma < 1$, every sweep shrinks the worst-case error by at least a factor of $\gamma$ — a mathematical **contraction mapping** guaranteed to converge to the unique true $V^*$ from *any* starting guess. Watch the values ripple backwards from the Vault one sweep at a time, and click any square to read its exact 4-action arithmetic.

<BellmanStepper />

<Deeper title="Why is Value Iteration guaranteed to converge?" tag="Maths">

Call one full Bellman sweep the operator $\mathcal{T}$: it maps a value vector $V$ to a new vector $\mathcal{T}V$. Take any two value vectors $U$ and $V$. At any state $s$, the difference between their best actions cannot exceed the largest difference on any single action:

$$
\big|(\mathcal{T}U)(s) - (\mathcal{T}V)(s)\big| \;\le\; \max_{a} \sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a)\,\gamma\,\big|U(s') - V(s')\big| \;\le\; \gamma\,\|U - V\|_\infty.
$$

Because $0 \le \gamma < 1$, each sweep multiplies the largest error anywhere on the grid by at most $\gamma$. After $k$ sweeps, the error is at most $\gamma^k \|V_0 - V^*\|_\infty \to 0$. By Banach's fixed-point theorem, $V^*$ is unique and Value Iteration always finds it.

</Deeper>

Real stochastic control policies — from spacecraft station-keeping to robot manipulation — exploit physical guardrails like this all the time.

## In code

Exact Value Iteration on our 6×4 Two-Treasure Cliff is 30 lines of plain Python with zero libraries:

```python
import math

ROWS, COLS = 4, 6
TERMINALS = {(3, 1): 2.0, (3, 2): -10.0, (3, 3): -10.0, (3, 4): -10.0, (2, 5): 10.0}
STEP_COST = -0.1
DIRS = [(-1, 0), (0, 1), (1, 0), (0, -1)]  # Up, Right, Down, Left

def transitions(r, c, a, slip):
    outcomes = [(a, 1 - slip), ((a - 1) % 4, slip / 2), ((a + 1) % 4, slip / 2)]
    res = []
    for d, p in outcomes:
        if p == 0: continue
        nr, nc = r + DIRS[d][0], c + DIRS[d][1]
        if not (0 <= nr < ROWS and 0 <= nc < COLS): nr, nc = r, c
        res.append((p, nr, nc))
    return res

def value_iteration(gamma=0.9, slip=0.2, tol=1e-10):
    V = [[0.0] * COLS for _ in range(ROWS)]
    for _ in range(500):
        delta, new_V = 0.0, [[0.0] * COLS for _ in range(ROWS)]
        for r in range(ROWS):
            for c in range(COLS):
                if (r, c) in TERMINALS: continue
                best = max(
                    sum(p * (TERMINALS[(nr, nc)] if (nr, nc) in TERMINALS
                             else STEP_COST + gamma * V[nr][nc])
                        for p, nr, nc in transitions(r, c, a, slip))
                    for a in range(4)
                )
                new_V[r][c] = best
                delta = max(delta, abs(best - V[r][c]))
        V = new_V
        if delta < tol: break
    return V

for label, g, s in [("Myopic  (g=0.5, slip=0.0)", 0.5, 0.0),
                    ("Patient (g=0.9, slip=0.0)", 0.9, 0.0),
                    ("Slip    (g=0.9, slip=0.2)", 0.9, 0.2)]:
    print(f"{label} -> V*(Start) = {value_iteration(g, s)[2][0]:.4f}")
```

<Misconception>

"Maximising immediate reward at every step maximises total reward." That is only true when $\gamma = 0$. In any sequential decision problem, an action that pays less right now — taking a negative step cost to walk past a `+2` gem, detouring around a slippery cliff, or even bouncing safely off a guardrail wall — can unlock a vastly higher future value $V^*(s')$. Value functions exist specifically to put immediate rewards and future consequences on the same scale.

</Misconception>

<Takeaways>

- A **Markov Decision Process (MDP)** formalises sequential decision-making as five pieces: states $\mathcal{S}$, actions $\mathcal{A}$, transitions $P(s' \mid s, a)$, rewards $R$, and a discount factor $\gamma$.
- The **discount factor** $\gamma$ controls the agent's planning horizon ($\approx 1/(1-\gamma)$ steps): small $\gamma$ makes the agent myopic; large $\gamma$ lets distant rewards outweigh nearby temptations.
- **Stochastic transitions** naturally produce risk-averse detours — and even surprising wall-guardrail tricks — directly from maximising expected discounted return.
- The **Bellman optimality equation**, $V^*(s) = \max_a \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)[R + \gamma V^*(s')]$, turns infinite-horizon planning into a one-step lookahead over cached neighbour values.
- **Value Iteration** repeatedly applies Bellman backups from $V_0 = 0$; because $\gamma < 1$ contracts error every sweep, it converges to the unique optimum $V^*$.

</Takeaways>
