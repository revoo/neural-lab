## The problem: learning without the simulator's source code

Value Iteration from the previous lesson felt almost like magic — give it a grid, and it instantly handed back the exact optimal policy. Yet it had one glaring catch: we had to hand it the entire **transition table** $P(s' \mid s, a)$ and **reward table** $R$ up front.

In real problems, nobody gives you the physics engine. You don't know ahead of time which corridor holds a pit, which ad headline converts best, or how a real robot arm responds to torque on a worn joint. You can only **try an action, watch what happens next, and update your beliefs**.

That creates two interlocking challenges:
1. **How do you balance trying new things against exploiting what already seems good?**
2. **How do you learn optimal behaviour from single `(state, action, reward, next state)` transitions — with no transition model $P$ at all?**

## First, the exploration–exploitation dilemma

Before adding a maze, strip the problem down to a single room with five slot machines (a **multi-armed bandit**). Each machine pays a random noisy reward around its own hidden average. You get 200 pulls to collect as much total reward as you can.

<Analogy title="Think of choosing restaurants in a new city">

On your first night in a new city you try a ramen shop and it's pretty good (`8/10`). On night two, you face the classic trade-off:
- **Exploit:** go straight back to the ramen shop you already know is good.
- **Explore:** try the taco place down the street, which might be a `4/10` disappointment — or a `10/10` revelation you would otherwise never discover.

Exploit too early and you settle for mediocrity forever. Explore forever and you waste half your nights eating bad food even after you've found the best restaurant in town.

</Analogy>

This is the **<Term id="exploration-exploitation">exploration–exploitation trade-off</Term>**. Four simple strategies capture the main ideas used across all of reinforcement learning:

- **Pure greedy ($\varepsilon = 0$):** always pull whichever machine has the highest sample average so far.
- **$\varepsilon$-greedy:** with small probability $\varepsilon$, pick a random machine; otherwise pick the current leader. (**Decaying $\varepsilon$** starts high and cools down over time.)
- **Optimistic initialisation:** start every machine's estimate artificially high (say `Q₀ = +4`). Every time you try a machine, reality disappoints your high expectation and drops its score, automatically driving you to try every untouched machine!
- **UCB1 (Upper Confidence Bound):** pick the machine maximising $\hat Q(a) + c\sqrt{\ln t / N(a)}$ — sample average *plus* an uncertainty bonus that shrinks as you collect more pulls $N(a)$.

Below, every strategy gets its own grid-searched best hyperparameter across **40 fixed seeds × 200 pulls** — and you can switch tabs to pull the 5 noisy machines by hand for 25 turns yourself.

<BanditExplorer />

Look at the honest gap in cumulative regret (how much reward each strategy left on the table compared to an oracle pulling the best machine every time): **Pure greedy** suffers **`94.9`** regret because it locks onto the true best arm only **29.8%** of the time. Even simple **$\varepsilon$-greedy** cuts regret in half (`47.0`), **decaying $\varepsilon$** (`32.0`) and **optimistic initialisation** (`31.3`) tie tightly, and **UCB1** wins decisively at **`20.2`** regret (**82.5%** optimal pulls) because it focuses its curiosity on genuinely uncertain arms rather than wasting random pulls on obvious losers.

## From states $V(s)$ to state–action pairs $Q(s, a)$

Now bring back the maze. Why couldn't we just learn state values $V(s)$ from experience and act greedily? Look back at Bellman's equation: to choose the best action using $V(s)$, you had to compute $\max_a \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)[R + \gamma V(s')]$ — which requires knowing $P(s' \mid s, a)$!

The fix is deceptively simple: **cache one value for every `(state, action)` pair**, called the **action-value** $Q(s, a)$ — *"how much total discounted return do I expect if I stand in state $s$, take action $a$ right now, and play optimally afterwards?"*

Once you have a table of $Q(s, a)$ values, picking the best move requires **zero knowledge of the environment's rules**: just pick $\arg\max_a Q(s, a)$.

How do we update $Q(s, a)$ from a single lived transition $(s, a) \to (r, s')$? Suppose your old guess was $Q(s, a)$. After actually taking action $a$, you observe real reward $r$ and land in $s'$, where your best estimate of the future is $\max_{a'} Q(s', a')$. So your improved estimate — the **TD target** — is $r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a')$. Subtract your old guess to get the **surprise** (the **<Term id="td-learning">Temporal-Difference error</Term>** $\delta$), and nudge $Q(s, a)$ a fraction $\alpha$ of the way toward the target:

<Eq
  words="\text{new } \ca{Q(s,a)} = \text{old } \ca{Q(s,a)} + \cp{\alpha}\Big(\,\underbrace{\cb{r} + \cp{\gamma}\max_{a'}\ca{Q(s',a')}}_{\text{TD target}} - \ca{Q(s,a)}\,\Big)"
  tex="\ca{Q(s, a)} \;\leftarrow\; \ca{Q(s, a)} + \cp{\alpha}\Big(\cb{r} + \cp{\gamma}\max_{a'}\ca{Q(s', a')} - \ca{Q(s, a)}\Big)"
>

- $\ca{Q(s, a)}$ — current estimate of taking action $a$ in state $s$ (the four triangles inside each square below)
- $\cp{\alpha}$ — **learning rate** in $(0, 1]$: how far to move toward each new observation (high $\alpha$ learns fast, low $\alpha$ averages out noise)
- $\cb{r}$ — the actual immediate reward observed on this step
- $\cp{\gamma}\max_{a'}\ca{Q(s', a')}$ — discounted value of the best action available in the *next* state $s'$ (`0` if $s'$ is terminal)
- $\cb{r} + \cp{\gamma}\max_{a'}\ca{Q(s', a')} - \ca{Q(s, a)}$ — the **TD error** $\delta$: positive if reality beat expectations, negative if it disappointed

</Eq>

That single line of arithmetic is **<Term id="q-learning">Q-learning</Term>** (Christopher Watkins, 1989). Notice what makes it extraordinary: you don't wait for an episode to finish, and you never estimate $P(s' \mid s, a)$. You **bootstrap** — updating a guess using another guess one step ahead — and real rewards slowly anchor the whole chain to the truth.

## Off-policy vs on-policy: Q-learning meets SARSA on the cliff

Look closely at the $\max_{a'} Q(s', a')$ inside Q-learning's update. While training, the agent is **not** always acting greedily — it is taking random $\varepsilon$-greedy exploration moves! Yet Q-learning updates its table assuming that from $s'$ onward it will act **perfectly greedily** ($\max_{a'}$).

That makes Q-learning **<Term id="off-policy">off-policy</Term>**: the policy it *evaluates* (greedy perfection) differs from the policy it actually *follows* during training ($\varepsilon$-greedy).

Its closest cousin, **<Term id="sarsa">SARSA</Term>** (State–Action–Reward–State–Action), changes **just one term**: instead of $\max_{a'} Q(s', a')$, it plugs in $Q(s', a')$ for the actual $\varepsilon$-greedy next action $a'$ the agent really chose!

<Eq
  words="\text{SARSA update: replace } \max_{a'} Q(s', a') \text{ with the } \mathbf{actual} \text{ next action } Q(s', a')"
  tex="\ca{Q(s, a)} \;\leftarrow\; \ca{Q(s, a)} + \cp{\alpha}\Big(\cb{r} + \cp{\gamma}\,\ca{Q(s', a')} - \ca{Q(s, a)}\Big)"
>

- $\ca{Q(s', a')}$ — value of the action $a'$ the agent *actually* takes next under its $\varepsilon$-greedy behaviour policy (making SARSA **on-policy**)

</Eq>

Does swapping one term actually change how the agent behaves? <Deeper title="Debrief: two surprising ways to beat the Shifting Goal" tag="Spoiler">

In **Brief 3 (Shifting Goal)**, once the old corner `(0,4)` downgrades from `+10` to `+2`, a neutral agent (`Q₀ = 0`) with a cooled-down **decaying $\varepsilon$** (`+3.82`) or tiny **`const 0.02`** (`+1.54`) gets stuck happily harvesting `+2` forever. With `Q₀ = 0`, you need **persistent exploration (`const 0.15`) + fast learning (`α = 0.6`)** to wander south and discover the new `+10` Vault (`+7.02`).

Yet our exhaustive search also surfaced a second valid mechanism: **optimistic initialisation (`Q₀ = +5`)**! Because the unvisited south squares still hold their initial `+5` optimism, the instant the north corner drops to `+2`, the agent's greedy policy pivots south on its own — hitting **`+9.66`**! Real-world non-stationary systems (such as news recommenders) rely on the same two tools: never letting exploration drop to zero, and giving unseen options an optimistic prior.

</Deeper>

## In code

Here is the complete Cliff Walking race in 38 lines of plain Python — notice how Q-learning and SARSA differ by literally one expression on the `boot = ...` line:

```python
import random

ROWS, COLS = 4, 8
DIRS = [(-1, 0), (0, 1), (1, 0), (0, -1)]

def step(r, c, a):
    nr, nc = max(0, min(ROWS - 1, r + DIRS[a][0])), max(0, min(COLS - 1, c + DIRS[a][1]))
    if nr == 3 and 1 <= nc <= 6:
        return 3, 0, -50.0, False   # cliff -> reset to Start!
    if (nr, nc) == (3, 7):
        return nr, nc, 0.0, True    # Goal!
    return nr, nc, -1.0, False

def eps_greedy(q_row, eps, rng):
    if rng.random() < eps:
        return rng.randrange(4)
    best = max(q_row)
    return rng.choice([a for a, v in enumerate(q_row) if v == best])

def train(algo, alpha=0.2, eps=0.1, gamma=0.99, episodes=400, seed=1):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    Q = [[0.0] * 4 for _ in range(ROWS * COLS)]
    late = []
    for ep in range(episodes):
        r, c = 3, 0
        s, a = r * COLS + c, eps_greedy(Q[r * COLS + c], eps, rng)
        ep_ret = 0.0
        for _ in range(120):
            nr, nc, rew, done = step(r, c, a)
            ep_ret += rew
            ns = nr * COLS + nc
            na = eps_greedy(Q[ns], eps, rng)
            boot = 0.0 if done else (max(Q[ns]) if algo == "qlearning" else Q[ns][na])
            Q[s][a] += alpha * (rew + gamma * boot - Q[s][a])
            r, c, s, a = nr, nc, ns, na
            if done: break
        if ep >= 160: late.append(ep_ret)
    return sum(late) / len(late)

for algo in ("qlearning", "sarsa"):
    avg = sum(train(algo, seed=s) for s in range(1, 21)) / 20
    print(f"{algo:10s} -> mean online return (ep 161-400, 20 seeds): {avg:.2f}")
```

Running it prints:


<Misconception>

"Q-learning is always better than SARSA because it finds the optimal policy." Only if you care exclusively about the *post-training greedy policy* and exploration mistakes during training are free. Whenever exploration stays active during real-world deployment — or when training failures carry real cost — on-policy SARSA's caution is a feature, not a bug.

</Misconception>

<Takeaways>

- Without a known transition model $P(s' \mid s, a)$, agents must learn from lived `(s, a, r, s')` transitions — and balance **exploration** against **exploitation**.
- On noisy bandits, **pure greedy** locks onto suboptimal arms (`94.9` regret); **UCB1** uses optimism under uncertainty to cut regret nearly 5× (`20.2`).
- Learning action values **$Q(s, a)$** instead of $V(s)$ lets an agent act greedily via $\arg\max_a Q(s, a)$ with zero transition model.
- **Temporal-Difference (TD) learning** bootstraps one step ahead: $Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha(r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') - Q(s,a))$.
- **Q-learning (off-policy)** targets the optimal greedy policy (wins post-training evaluation), while **SARSA (on-policy)** prices in its own exploration moves (wins safe online training on the cliff).
- In **non-stationary** worlds where rewards move, cooling $\varepsilon$ to zero can trap an agent on an obsolete reward forever.

</Takeaways>

<InTheWild>

Tabular Q-learning works brilliantly for 32 grid squares — and completely collapses when a state is an Atari video frame (`210 × 160 × 3` pixels) or a 3D robot joint vector. In 2013–2015, DeepMind's **DQN (Deep Q-Network)** replaced the Q-table with a neural network $Q_\theta(s, a)$, stabilising TD bootstrapping with two tricks: an **experience replay buffer** (breaking correlations between consecutive frames) and a slowly updated **target network** (stopping the TD target from chasing its own tail). Yet value-based methods still hit a hard wall whenever actions are *continuous* (like steering angles) or there are 100,000 vocabulary tokens — because you can't easily take $\max_a Q(s, a)$ over an infinite or huge action space.

</InTheWild>
