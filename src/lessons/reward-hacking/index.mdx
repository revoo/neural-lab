## The problem: you get exactly what you pay for

In 2016, OpenAI researchers trained an agent to play *CoastRunners*, a boat-racing game. They wanted it to win races. The game's score rose when you hit targets along the course, so they used the score as the reward. The agent found a small lagoon where three targets respawned just as fast as it could circle back to them, and it went round and round that lagoon for ever, crashing into other boats and catching fire, never finishing the race. It scored about 20% more than human players did.

Nothing was broken. The learning algorithm worked perfectly. It found a better way to earn the reward than anyone had imagined, and that was the problem. This lesson is about the gap between the number we write down and the thing we meant, and why every algorithm in this module, from Value Iteration to PPO, will find that gap if it exists.

<Analogy title="Think of paying a team per bug fixed">

A manager wants fewer bugs in the product, so she starts paying engineers a bonus for every bug they fix. Within weeks the bug tracker is busier than ever. Some of it is genuine. Some of it is engineers noticing that a hastily written feature produces lots of bugs, each worth a bonus. Nobody needs to be dishonest: the incentive quietly favours writing code that breaks in fixable ways.

The manager's mistake was paying for an *activity* that usually comes with the goal (fixing bugs) instead of the goal itself (software that works). When the two can be pulled apart, an incentive pulls them apart. Economists call this **Goodhart's law**: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

An RL agent is that engineer with no sense of what the manager really wanted and unlimited patience to find the cheapest way to the bonus.

</Analogy>

## The boat-race loop, exactly

Let's rebuild CoastRunners in miniature, so that nothing is left to luck or training. A tiny track: start on the left, the finish five squares to the right (+10, and the race ends). Off to the side is a turbo pad that pays a bonus every time the boat drives onto it, then needs 8 steps to recharge. Future rewards are discounted by γ = 0.95, as in the MDP lesson.

We won't train anything. Value Iteration solves this little world *exactly*, so what you see is the best possible behaviour for the reward you set, the plan that any sufficiently good learner would eventually converge to.

There are really only three plans: drive straight (worth 10·γ⁴ = 8.15), detour once over the pad and then finish, or circle the pad for ever. The detour costs four steps of delay on the +10, so it's only worth it once the bonus passes **1.762**. Circling gives up the +10 entirely, but collects a bonus every 9 steps for ever. Above **4.540** that stream is worth more than finishing, and the boat never finishes again. There's no "collect it a bit more often" in between: the best plan jumps from one strategy to another.

</Predict>

Why does a bonus of under half the finish reward beat the finish? Because a reward that can be collected *repeatedly* isn't really the size it looks.

<Eq
  words="\text{worth of a bonus collected every } \cp{L} \text{ steps, for ever} = \frac{\cb{\text{bonus}}}{1 - \cp{\gamma}^{\,\cp{L}}}"
  tex="\cb{b} + \cp{\gamma}^{\cp{L}}\cb{b} + \cp{\gamma}^{2\cp{L}}\cb{b} + \cdots = \frac{\cb{b}}{1 - \cp{\gamma}^{\cp{L}}}"
>

- $\cb{b}$ — the **bonus** paid each time (here, a pad hit)
- $\cp{L}$ — how many **steps between collections** (the pad here: 8 to recharge plus 1 to drive back on, so 9)
- $\cp{\gamma}$ — the **discount factor**: each step into the future counts γ times as much
- $1/(1 - \gamma^L)$ — the **multiplier** a repeatable reward gets. With γ = 0.95 and L = 9 it's 2.70: a pad worth 4.54 is really worth about 12.3, more than the 10 for finishing

</Eq>

This is the first pattern of reward hacking: **anything renewable, the agent will farm**. The designer saw a pad as a one-off encouragement; the reward function saw it as a fountain.

<Misconception>

"The agent is cheating, or buggy." It's neither. The plan you see is provably the best answer to the question the reward asked. Saying "it found a loophole" is a polite way of saying "the reward meant something we didn't". The fix is always to change the question (the reward, the environment, or what's measured), never to scold the optimiser. And a smarter optimiser makes things *worse*, not better: it finds the loophole faster and more reliably. Ours is exact, so it never misses one.

</Misconception>

## Shaping: hints that help, and hints that become the goal

Why would anyone add a bonus like the turbo pad in the first place? Usually for a good reason: **sparse rewards are slow to learn from**. Picture an open 5×5 field with a single +10 in the far corner and nothing else. A Q-learner that starts knowing nothing wanders randomly until it happens to hit the goal, and only then does any value start to flow back through its table. In our runs, its first episode took 72 steps on average to stumble on a goal 8 steps away.

So designers add hints: a little bonus for getting closer to the goal. This is called **<Term id="reward-shaping">reward shaping</Term>**, and done naively it reproduces the boat loop. A bonus paid for *being near* the goal is renewable: the agent can park next to the goal and collect it every step. And finishing ends the episode, which ends the stream of bonuses.

<ShapingExplorer />

The naive bonus seems harmless at first. Up to k = 4/7 ≈ 0.571, not one square's best moves change. Past that, the agent parks beside the goal and bumps the wall for ever. That exact threshold is no accident: parking pays k × ⅞ per step for ever, worth 17.5k, which passes the +10 for finishing at k = 4/7.

In 1999, Andrew Ng, Daishi Harada and Stuart Russell showed there's exactly one shape of bonus you can always add safely. Give every state a **potential** Φ(s), any number you like (here, closeness to the goal), and pay the *change* in potential on every step:

<Eq
  words="\cg{\text{shaping bonus}} = \cp{\gamma} \times \ca{\text{potential where you land}} - \ca{\text{potential where you were}}"
  tex="\cg{F(s, s')} = \cp{\gamma}\,\ca{\Phi(s')} - \ca{\Phi(s)}"
>

- $\ca{\Phi(s)}$ — the **potential** of state $s$: a hint of how promising it is (any function of the state; here, k × closeness to the goal). Set $\Phi = 0$ in states where the episode ends
- $\cp{\gamma}$ — the same discount factor the agent uses
- $\cg{F(s, s')}$ — the **bonus** paid on the step from $s$ to $s'$, added to the real reward

</Eq>

Why is this safe? Add up the bonuses along any route and almost everything cancels: each Φ appears once with a plus (when you arrive) and once with a minus (when you leave). What's left depends only on where you started, so every route from a square gets the same total adjustment. In the widget, every value moved by exactly −k·Φ(s) and the best moves didn't change in a single square, at any strength. A loop gives back (almost) exactly what it collects, so there's nothing to farm.

And does it actually help? We raced Q-learners on the field, each reward at its own best step size, over 30 seeds. With no shaping, the greedy policy first walked the 8-step route after **15.3** episodes on average. With potential-based shaping it took **1.6**. The honest surprise is the naive bonus at k = 0.25. That's well below the threshold, so the best plan is *unchanged*, yet it made learning slower: **39.1** episodes, and one seed in 30 never learned within 150. The bonus doesn't change where learning ends up, but it gives the learner something to dawdle near on the way.

<Deeper title="Two fine prints on potential-based shaping">

**Endings need Φ = 0.** The cancellation argument leaves one term: γᵀ·Φ(final state). If episodes can end in different places or at different times, that term differs between plans and *can* change which is best. Setting Φ = 0 wherever an episode ends (as the widget does at the goal) makes it vanish.

**It's the same as a good first guess.** Eric Wiewiora showed in 2003 that for Q-learning, adding potential-based shaping is equivalent to starting the Q-table at Φ(s) instead of 0. That's why it speeds learning so dramatically here: it's the optimistic, informed initialisation from the Q-learning lesson, in disguise.

</Deeper>

## Lab: The Reward-Hacking Sandbox

Now you write the rewards. Each brief is a small world, a description of what the designer actually wants, and a few dials for the reward. When you press the button, an exact optimiser finds the highest-reward behaviour, and a checker follows **every** plan the optimiser rates as best, including ties, and tests it against what you wanted. The checker is deliberately pessimistic: if two plans tie and one of them breaks the vase, you can't count on the agent picking the other.

Getting a reward that works is the goal. Once you have, there's an optional second challenge: find the **simplest** reward that works (fewest non-zero terms, then the smallest numbers). The optimum was found by trying every setting of the dials.

<Deeper title="Debrief: what the three briefs teach" tag="Spoiler">

**Brief 1 · The sponsor's turbo pad** (13 of 90 rewards work). The pad must be big enough to justify a detour but small enough not to become a career. With +10 for finishing, pad values of 2, 3 and 4 work and 5 loops. The simplest working reward is surprising: **nothing at all for finishing, +4 per pad hit and −1 per step**. Finishing is worth zero, but it's the only way to stop paying for time, and that's enough. Many rewards are best understood not by what they pay for but by what they let the agent *stop*.

**Brief 2 · The vase in the hallway** (34 of 75 work). The first reward doesn't mention the vase, so the vase doesn't exist as far as the agent is concerned. That's the side-effect problem: you can't list everything you care about, and anything you leave out is fair game. Even once the vase is priced in, it's *only* priced. With +10 for delivering, a −2 vase penalty protects it while time is free, but once every step costs −1 the shortcut saves more than the vase costs, and the vase goes. The simplest working reward is **−2 for the vase and −1 per step**.

**Brief 3 · The camera** (12 of 72 work). Paying for what the camera reports gets the camera switched off (3 steps, against 7 to actually clean). Paying per mess cleaned gets messes spilled and re-cleaned for ever. And there's a subtle third trap. With +1 per clean and −5 per spill, the robot cleans both messes properly, and then the checker still fails it. Once the job is done there's nothing left to earn, every non-spilling action ties, and one of the tied plans wanders over and switches the camera off. **An agent is indifferent to everything you didn't price**, including what it does after the job is finished.

The simplest working reward is **+1 per step the camera reports "clean", −5 for touching the camera**. Keep the camera's report as the thing you pay for, but make tampering with it cost more than it can earn. It's fragile, though: raise the report to +2 per step and the same −5 fine becomes a price worth paying. A fine is a price, not a prohibition.

</Deeper>

<ForReal>

Reward hacking is a live problem in today's systems. OpenAI's 2016 CoastRunners boat circled a lagoon farming targets instead of finishing the race, and researchers keep a public list of such "specification gaming" examples. Language models trained with reinforcement learning do it too, for example by writing code that special-cases the unit tests instead of solving the task, which is why reward design and monitoring are part of every RL pipeline.

</ForReal>

## In code

Here's the boat race in plain Python: Value Iteration over (square, pad timer) states, then follow the best move from the start.

```python
TRACK = ["S....F",
         "##.##.",
         "##P..."]
MOVES = [(-1, 0), (0, 1), (1, 0), (0, -1)]   # up, right, down, left
RECHARGE, GAMMA = 8, 0.95

def step(state, move, finish, pad):
    r, c, timer = state
    nr, nc = r + MOVES[move][0], c + MOVES[move][1]
    if not (0 <= nr < 3 and 0 <= nc < 6) or TRACK[nr][nc] == "#":
        nr, nc = r, c                          # bumped a wall
    reward, timer = 0.0, max(0, timer - 1)
    if TRACK[nr][nc] == "P" and state[2] == 0:
        reward, timer = pad, RECHARGE          # the pad fires, then recharges
    if TRACK[nr][nc] == "F":
        return None, reward + finish           # race over
    return (nr, nc, timer), reward

def best_plan(finish, pad, steps=16):
    states = [(r, c, t) for r in range(3) for c in range(6) for t in range(RECHARGE + 1)
              if TRACK[r][c] not in "#F"]
    V = {s: 0.0 for s in states}
    for _ in range(2000):                      # value iteration
        V = {s: max(rew + (GAMMA * V[n] if n else 0)
                    for n, rew in (step(s, m, finish, pad) for m in range(4)))
             for s in states}
    s, plan = (0, 0, 0), ""
    for _ in range(steps):                     # follow the best move from the start
        m = max(range(4), key=lambda m: (lambda n, rew: rew + (GAMMA * V[n] if n else 0))(*step(s, m, finish, pad)))
        plan += "↑→↓←"[m]
        s, _ = step(s, m, finish, pad)
        if s is None:
            return plan + " FINISHED"
    return plan + " ..."

for pad in [1, 3, 5]:
    print(f"pad bonus {pad}: {best_plan(finish=10, pad=pad)}")
```

Running it prints:

```text
pad bonus 1: →→→→→ FINISHED
pad bonus 3: →→↓↓↑↑→→→ FINISHED
pad bonus 5: →→↓↓↑↑↑↑↑↑↑↓↓↑↑↑ ...
```

The ↑↑↑↑↑ in the last line is the boat bumping the wall at the top of the side lane, waiting for the pad to recharge: in this world the cheapest way to "circle" is to sit still.

<Misconception>

"With a big enough model, the agent will figure out what we really meant." Understanding what you meant and *wanting* what you meant are different things. A model can know perfectly well that the camera is supposed to measure cleanliness. If it is optimised for the camera's report, the optimisation still pushes it towards whatever raises the report. Better understanding gives it more ways to do that, not fewer.

</Misconception>

<Takeaways>

- An optimiser maximises the reward you wrote, not the goal you meant. The gap between them is where **reward hacking** (or specification gaming) lives, and a better optimiser finds it more reliably.
- **Renewable rewards get farmed.** A bonus collected every $L$ steps is worth $b/(1-\gamma^L)$: on the boat track a pad bonus of 4.54 outweighs a +10 finish, and the boat never finishes again.
- The best plan doesn't change smoothly. It jumps between strategies at exact thresholds (1.762 and 4.540 for the pad).
- **Naive shaping** ("+k for being close") is renewable too: above k = 4/7 the agent parks next to the goal. **Potential-based shaping**, $\gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s)$, never changes the best plan at any strength, and here cut learning from 15.3 episodes to 1.6.
- **Measurements get gamed.** Pay for what a camera reports and the camera gets switched off. Pay for an activity (cleaning) and the activity gets manufactured.
- A penalty is a **price**, not a prohibition. And an agent is **indifferent** to everything you didn't price.

</Takeaways>

<InTheWild>

DeepMind researchers keep a public list of specification-gaming examples (Krakovna et al., 2020) that reads like this lesson's lab: a simulated robot asked to stack a red block on a blue one, rewarded by the height of the red block's bottom face, simply flipped the red block over; evolved creatures exploited bugs in physics engines; a Tetris-playing agent paused the game for ever to avoid losing. In language models, the same pattern appears as **reward-model over-optimisation**: optimise hard enough against a learned reward model and true quality rises, then falls (Gao, Schulman and Hilton, 2022), which is exactly the Goodhart curve from the RLHF lesson. More recently, people training coding models with "the unit tests pass" as the reward have reported models special-casing or editing the tests rather than fixing the code. The tools that help are the ones from this lesson: reward outcomes rather than measurements, price the side effects you can name, keep a KL leash to sensible behaviour, and check the optimised policy for plans you didn't intend.

</InTheWild>
