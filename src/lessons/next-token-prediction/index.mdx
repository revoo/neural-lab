## The problem: how does a stack of blocks write?

By the end of Module 12 you had the entire transformer sitting on the bench: a tokeniser that chops text into integer IDs, an embedding table that turns each ID into a vector, rotary positions, multi-head attention with a causal mask, residual connections, layer norm, and feed-forward layers.

Now feed in a prompt — say, `"The capital of France is"` — and watch what comes out the far end. Not a finished paragraph. Not an answer. Just one vector per input token, and at the final position, one linear layer turning that vector into a list of raw scores (logits), one for every token in the vocabulary. Pass those scores through softmax and you have a single probability distribution: **the model's guess for the very next token.**

How does guessing *one* next token turn into an assistant that writes essays, debugs Python and explains quantum mechanics?

<Analogy title="Think of a master improviser playing a one-word-at-a-time game">

Suppose you play a game where you can only ever add **one word** to a sentence in progress. Given `"The animal didn't cross the street because it was too"`, you don't know the whole story yet, so you assign probabilities: maybe 35% `"tired"`, 18% `"wide"`, 10% `"dark"`, and tiny slices to thousands of other words.

You pick one word, write it onto the page, read the whole slightly longer page back, and do it again. There is no separate "outline generator" or "essay writer". Writing a whole page is just playing the one-word game hundreds of times in a row.

</Analogy>

That loop — predict a distribution for the next token, pick one, append it to the prompt, feed the longer prompt back in — is called **<Term id="autoregressive">autoregressive generation</Term>**, and a neural network trained to produce those distributions is a **<Term id="language-model">language model</Term>**.

Try stepping through the loop yourself on a real neural language model we trained right inside this page (1,611 parameters, 61-word vocabulary, 2-word context window).

<AutoregressiveLoop />

Notice two things immediately:

- **There is no separate "generator".** The exact same forward pass that scores a prompt also writes the reply — just called once per generated token. That is also why LLM responses stream out word by word: token 50 cannot be computed until token 49 has been chosen and fed back in.
- **Predicting well forces understanding.** It is tempting to dismiss next-token prediction as "glorified autocomplete". Try finishing `"The animal didn't cross the street because it was too ___"` or `"def fib(n): return 1 if n <= 1 else ___"` or `"In 1969, the first astronauts landed on the ___"`. To put high probability on the right next token across trillions of web pages, books and code repositories, a model *has* to internalise syntax, facts, logic and code structure. You cannot compress text well without understanding what generated it.

## How do we score a language model? Perplexity

We already know the training loss from <LessonLink to="information">the logarithms and cross-entropy lesson</LessonLink>: at every token $t$, look at the probability $p(x_t \mid x_{<t})$ the model assigned to the token that *actually* came next, take its surprise $-\ln p$, and average across the text.

Average cross-entropy is great for gradient descent, but `"loss = 1.50 nats"` is hard to feel in your bones. Is 1.50 good? How much better is 1.20 than 1.50?

Exponentiate it.

<Eq
  words="\text{effective number of equally likely choices} = e^{\,\text{average surprise per token}}"
  tex="\cp{\text{PPL}} = e^{\ca{H}} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{\cb{N}}\sum_{t=1}^{\cb{N}} \ln \cc{p}(x_t \mid x_{<t})\right)"
>

- $\cc{p}(x_t \mid x_{<t})$ — the **probability** the model gave the true token $x_t$, given all earlier tokens $x_{<t}$
- $\ca{H}$ — the **average cross-entropy loss** in nats: mean surprise $-\ln p$ per token
- $\cb{N}$ — the **number of tokens** scored
- $\cp{\text{PPL}}$ — **<Term id="perplexity">perplexity</Term>**: the size of the fair die that would leave you just as surprised on average

</Eq>

Why is $e^H$ so intuitive? Check the simplest cases:

- **Fair coin** ($p = 1/2$ every step): $H = \ln 2 \approx 0.693$, so $\text{PPL} = e^{\ln 2} = \mathbf{2}$.
- **Fair 6-sided die** ($p = 1/6$ every step): $H = \ln 6 \approx 1.792$, so $\text{PPL} = \mathbf{6}$.
- **Uniform guessing over our 61-word vocabulary** ($p = 1/61$): $\text{PPL} = \mathbf{61}$.
- **Perfect oracle** ($p = 1$ every step): $H = 0$, so $\text{PPL} = \mathbf{1}$.

Perplexity turns an abstract logarithm back into an **effective branching factor**: "out of $V$ possible tokens, how many equally likely choices does the model effectively narrow each step down to?"

It **more than doubles**, from **1.14** to **2.83**. The four great words cost only $0.22 + 0.11 + 0.16 + 0.11 = 0.60$ nats combined; the single 1% blunder costs $-\ln(0.01) = 4.61$ nats all by itself! Averaging over five tokens gives $H = 1.04$ nats and $e^{1.04} = 2.83$.

That asymmetric punishment is why cross-entropy trains well-calibrated models: putting 0% on a plausible word is fatal, so a model quickly learns to spread probability honestly wherever genuine ambiguity exists.

## Watching a real language model learn

Our mini-corpus has 30 short training sentences (268 tokens, 61 distinct words including `<start>`), 3 validation sentences (for tuning baselines fairly), and 4 held-out test sentences (37 tokens) containing brand-new word combinations never seen during training — like `"the fox watched"` and `"the dog hid"`.

Before training the neural network, let's build three fair baselines on the exact same held-out test sentences (each n-gram baseline's Laplace smoothing $\alpha$ was chosen on the validation split, never peeking at test):

| Model | What it uses | Held-out test perplexity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Uniform** | Nothing ($1/61$ each) | **61.0** |
| **Unigram** | Word frequencies only ($\alpha = 0.01$) | **32.8** |
| **Bigram** | Last 1 word lookup table ($\alpha = 0.01$) | **5.57** |
| **Tiny neural LM** | Last 2 words → embeddings (6D) → 16 tanh → softmax (1,611 params) | **4.50** (avg **4.74** over 6 seeds) |

Press **Play training** and watch perplexity fall from random guessing (~62) all the way past the bigram baseline, while the model's generated text turns from pure word salad into coherent sentences.

<LiveTrainingPpl />

Two honest observations from running the real numbers — not just the textbook slogan:

1. **Why neural embeddings beat lookup tables on unseen pairs.** Look at the unseen test phrase `"the fox watched"`. In training, `fox` only ever `ran` and `slept`, while `cat` and `dog` `watched`, `drank` and `chased`. A bigram count table treats every word ID as an isolated bucket, so `fox → watched` gets a tiny smoothing floor of **0.28%** — driving the bigram's perplexity on `"the fox watched the small mouse in the yard ."` all the way up to **13.3**. Our neural model maps `fox`, `cat` and `dog` to nearby vectors in its 6-dimensional embedding space, assigns **2.16%** to `watched` (~8× higher!) and lands at perplexity **5.53**. Across all six random seeds we tested (`1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7`), the neural model beat the validation-tuned bigram every single time (**4.45–5.15**, average **4.74** vs **5.57**).
2. **Where the lookup table still wins.** Look honestly at test sentence #4 (`"a bright moon shone over the quiet house ."`): every single adjacent word pair in that sentence happened to appear in training. There, the simple bigram lookup table gets **2.84** and actually *beats* our tiny 120-epoch neural net (**4.48**). Neural language models win because real language is overwhelmingly made of novel combinations that no lookup table can ever store — Yoshua Bengio's classic 2003 insight that kicked off neural language modelling.

## In code

Computing perplexity and running the autoregressive generation loop take a dozen lines of plain Python:

```python
import math
import random

def perplexity(probs_of_true_tokens):
    """Effective number of equally likely choices: exp(average cross-entropy)."""
    avg_loss = -sum(math.log(max(p, 1e-12)) for p in probs_of_true_tokens) / len(probs_of_true_tokens)
    return math.exp(avg_loss)

print("fair 6-sided die: ", round(perplexity([1 / 6] * 5), 2))                    # 6.0
print("confident & right:", round(perplexity([0.8, 0.9, 0.85, 0.9, 0.95]), 2))  # 1.14
print("one 1% blunder:   ", round(perplexity([0.8, 0.9, 0.85, 0.9, 0.01]), 2))  # 2.83

def generate(prompt_ids, next_token_probs, steps, rng):
    ids = list(prompt_ids)
    for _ in range(steps):
        probs = next_token_probs(ids)                   # forward pass -> distribution over V tokens
        nxt = rng.choices(range(len(probs)), weights=probs, k=1)[0]
        ids.append(nxt)                                 # feed it back as part of the prompt
    return ids
```

In a real transformer, `next_token_probs(ids)` runs the whole stack of blocks and takes softmax at the final position — and uses a **KV cache** (covered in Module 15) so it doesn't recompute keys and values for earlier tokens on every iteration of that `for` loop.

<Misconception>

"Lower perplexity always means a smarter, more helpful assistant." Perplexity only measures how well a model predicts the distribution of *its evaluation text*. A model with great perplexity on Wikipedia can still refuse to follow instructions, hallucinate citations, or repeat itself in a loop when generating. Pretraining minimises perplexity; turning a base predictor into a useful assistant requires post-training (instruction tuning and preference alignment, Module 14) and careful **sampling** — the subject of the next lesson. Also beware comparing perplexity numbers across different tokenisers: splitting words into smaller tokens makes each individual token easier to predict, lowering per-token perplexity without making the model any better at predicting whole text!

</Misconception>

<Takeaways>

- A transformer generates text **autoregressively**: predict a probability distribution over all $V$ tokens for the next position, pick one token, append it to the prompt, and repeat.
- **Perplexity** ($\text{PPL} = e^H$) turns average cross-entropy loss back into an intuitive **effective branching factor** — the size of the fair die that would leave you equally surprised.
- Because cross-entropy uses $-\ln p$, a single overconfident blunder (e.g. $p = 1\%$) penalises perplexity far more than hedging does.
- **Neural language models** beat count-based lookup tables on unseen word combinations because learned embeddings share statistical strength across similar words (`cat`, `dog`, `fox`).
- Perplexity is only comparable across models that share the **same tokeniser**, and great perplexity on prose is the start of an assistant, not the finish line.

</Takeaways>

<InTheWild>

In 2003, Yoshua Bengio and colleagues published *A Neural Probabilistic Language Model* — essentially the exact embedding-plus-MLP architecture you just trained in this lesson — showing that learned word embeddings broke the "curse of dimensionality" that crippled n-gram tables. Sixteen years later, GPT-2 scaled that same next-token objective onto 1.5 billion transformer parameters and pushed WikiText-2 perplexity down to ~18 (out of 50,257 subword tokens). Modern frontier models push English perplexity into single digits, yet the core training loop is still unchanged: predict the next token, compute $-\ln p(\text{true})$, backpropagate, repeat trillions of times.
