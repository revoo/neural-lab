# RLHF & Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO)

A freshly pretrained language model has read much of the internet, yet it is a terrible assistant. Ask it *"Explain why the sky is blue"* and it might continue with *"Explain why the grass is green. Exercise 3: …"* because it was trained only to predict the next token of internet documents.

**Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)** on curated question–answer pairs gets you surprisingly far — yet SFT only tells the model *one* acceptable way to answer. It never explicitly teaches the model what *not* to say, nor how to rank a crisp, accurate explanation above a sycophantic wall of fluff or a plausible-sounding myth.

That is where **preference alignment** comes in: **<Term id="rlhf">RLHF</Term>** (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) and its remarkably clean mathematical cousin, **<Term id="dpo">Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO)</Term>**.

---

## 1. Why preferences beat scores — and why RLHF needs a KL leash

Ask ten humans to rate an assistant reply from 1 to 10 and they disagree wildly (one person’s 7 is another person’s 9). Show them **two** replies side by side and ask *"Which one is better? ($y_w \succ y_l$)"*, and agreement jumps sharply.

The classic **RLHF** pipeline turns those pairwise votes into an assistant in two steps:

1. **Train a proxy reward model $\hat{r}_\phi(x, y)$** using the **Bradley–Terry model** — the exact same probability rule used for chess Elo ratings:
   $$P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) \;=\; \sigma\!\big(\hat{r}_\phi(x, y_w) - \hat{r}_\phi(x, y_l)\big).$$
2. **Optimise the language model $\pi_\theta$ to score high on $\hat{r}_\phi$ — while keeping it on a leash** so it cannot wander arbitrarily far from the SFT reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$:

<Eq
  words="RLHF objective  =  expected proxy reward  −  leash strength β  ×  KL distance from SFT reference"
  tex="\max_{\pi} \;\; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}\!\big[\cb{\hat{r}(x, y)}\big] \;-\; \cp{\beta}\, \ca{D_{\text{KL}}\!\big(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\big)}"
  legend={[
    ['\\hat{r}(x, y)', 'score predicted by the learned proxy reward model', 'b'],
    ['\\beta', 'KL penalty strength — larger β = tighter leash to the SFT reference', 'p'],
    ['D_{\\text{KL}}(\\pi \\|\\, \\pi_{\\text{ref}})', 'how far the aligned policy has drifted from the starting SFT model (in nats)', 'a'],
  ]}
/>

Why do we need that KL leash $\beta$? Because of **Goodhart’s Law**: *when a learned proxy score becomes the target of intense optimisation, the optimiser finds glitches that score huge on the proxy while being awful to real humans* — **<Term id="reward-hacking">reward hacking</Term>**.

Look at that inverted-U curve: it reproduces from first-principles algebra the exact **over-optimisation curve** observed in frontier RLHF runs (Gao et al., 2022). ## 2. The DPO trick: cancelling out the reward model completely

Classical RLHF is notoriously tricky to engineer: you must train a separate reward model, hold four large models in GPU memory during PPO (policy, reference, reward model, value critic), and tune fragile RL hyperparameters.

In 2023, Rafailov et al. noticed something astonishing about the exact closed-form optimum we just plotted:

$$\pi^*(y \mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\, \exp\!\left(\frac{r(x, y)}{\beta}\right).$$

Take the logarithm of both sides and solve for $r(x, y)$:

$$r(x, y) \;=\; \beta \log\frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} \;+\; \beta \log Z(x).$$

Now substitute that expression into the Bradley–Terry preference probability $P(y_w \succ y_l) = \sigma(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l))$. Look what happens to the ugly, intractable normalisation term $\beta \log Z(x)$: because $Z(x)$ depends only on the prompt $x$ and **not** on the reply $y$, it **cancels out completely**!

What remains is **Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO)** — a single supervised loss directly on the language model $\pi_\theta$, with **no reward model and no RL loop**:

<Eq
  words="DPO loss  =  −log σ( implicit reward of winner  −  implicit reward of loser )"
  tex="\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) \;=\; -\log \sigma\!\left( \cp{\beta}\ca{\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)}} \;-\; \cp{\beta}\cb{\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}} \right)"
  legend={[
    ['\\beta', 'same KL leash parameter as RLHF — controls how far π_θ may drift from π_ref', 'p'],
    ['\\log(\\pi_\\theta(y_w)/\\pi_{\\text{ref}}(y_w))', 'how much more (or less) likely the policy makes the chosen reply than SFT did', 'a'],
    ['\\log(\\pi_\\theta(y_l)/\\pi_{\\text{ref}}(y_l))', 'how much more (or less) likely the policy makes the rejected reply than SFT did', 'b'],
  ]}
/>

Take its derivative and you uncover why DPO feels so natural: the gradient pushes $\log \pi_\theta(y_w)$ **up** and $\log \pi_\theta(y_l)$ **down**, multiplied by a self-weighting factor **$\sigma(-\Delta)$**! When the model ranks a pair **backwards** ($\Delta < 0$), $\sigma(-\Delta)$ approaches 1 (push hard!); once the model already clearly prefers $y_w$ ($\Delta \gg 0$), $\sigma(-\Delta)$ fades toward 0 (leave it alone!).

<Deeper title="DPO vs online RL (PPO / GRPO): an honest trade-off">
If DPO is so much simpler than PPO, why do frontier labs still run online RL (PPO, GRPO) for reasoning models? Because **DPO is offline**: it learns only from a fixed pre-collected dataset of $(y_w, y_l)$ pairs. As the policy improves, it starts generating *new* mistakes that never appeared in the offline preference file. Online RLHF — and verifiable-reward RL like GRPO on maths and code — continually samples fresh outputs from the *current* policy and scores them on the fly. Both methods share the exact same KL-regularised core mathematics you just explored!
</Deeper>

## 3. Why rejecting losers beats SFT on winners alone

You might wonder: if we have clean pairs $(y_w, y_l)$, why not just run ordinary supervised fine-tuning on the winners $y_w$ and throw away the losers $y_l$?

Compare both live below on our 8-reply problem. SFT raises the winners — which passively steals probability mass from everything else — yet it never specifically targets the dangerous sycophantic hacks. **DPO** actively pushes rejected hacks downward, slashing hack probability much further while achieving higher true helpfulness at similar KL distance.

## 5. Twelve lines of Python for DPO’s loss & self-weighting gradient

You can verify our exact DPO implicit rewards, margin, loss, and self-weighting factor $\sigma(-\Delta)$ in twelve lines of plain Python:

```python
import math

def sigmoid(x):
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-x))

def dpo_loss_and_weight(logp_w, logref_w, logp_l, logref_l, beta=0.5):
    rw = beta * (logp_w - logref_w)
    rl = beta * (logp_l - logref_l)
    margin = rw - rl
    loss = math.log1p(math.exp(-margin))
    grad_weight = sigmoid(-margin)
    return rw, rl, margin, loss, grad_weight

# Case 1: policy currently prefers rejected over chosen (wrong!)
print("Wrong :", [round(v, 3) for v in dpo_loss_and_weight(-3.0, -2.0, -1.0, -2.0, beta=0.5)])
# Case 2: policy strongly prefers chosen over rejected (already aligned)
print("Right :", [round(v, 3) for v in dpo_loss_and_weight(-0.5, -2.0, -4.5, -2.0, beta=0.5)])
```

Running it prints `[r_w, r_l, margin, loss, grad_weight]`:

```text
Wrong : [-0.5, 0.5, -1.0, 1.313, 0.731]
Right : [0.75, -1.25, 2.0, 0.127, 0.119]
```

Notice how the gradient weight drops **6×** (from `0.731` down to `0.119`) once the model already prefers the chosen answer!
