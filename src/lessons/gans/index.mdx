## The problem: why pixel averages look blurry

In <LessonLink to="autoencoders">autoencoders and VAEs</LessonLink>, we saw why training a generator with squared pixel error tends to produce slightly soft, blurry samples: whenever two sharp outputs are equally plausible, the average sitting between them has lower squared error than guessing either one.

In June 2014, Ian Goodfellow and seven colleagues at the Université de Montréal proposed a radically different training signal: **throw away pixel-by-pixel reconstruction error and let a second neural network judge realism instead.** <Src href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661" /> <Src href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_adversarial_network" />

<Analogy title="A forger and a detective">

Picture an art forger (the **generator**, $G$) trying to paint convincing banknotes from random scribbles of noise $z$, and an art detective (the **discriminator**, $D$) whose sole job is to separate genuine bills from forgeries.

At first the forger produces crude blobs and the detective spots them instantly. Every time the detective points out a telltale flaw — "real bills never sit in that empty gap; their texture is sharper" — the forger adjusts its brush strokes to erase that flaw. Every time the forger gets sharper, the detective studies finer details. Neither network ever computes a blurry average: a blurry banknote is an instant giveaway, so the detective forces the forger onto sharp, realistic modes.

</Analogy>

This adversarial setup is a **<Term id="gan">Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)</Term>**: two ordinary neural networks trained simultaneously in a **<Term id="minimax-game">minimax game</Term>** where one network's gain is the other's loss. <Src href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661" />

## Watching a real 1D duel live

Below is a real GAN running in your browser: both the generator $G$ and the discriminator $D$ are small multilayer perceptrons (`14 → 14` hidden units with `tanh`), seeded and trained with exact backpropagation through $D(G(z))$. The real world is a 1D mixture of three sharp Gaussian peaks at $-2.4$, $0$ and $+2.4$. The forger starts as a clumsy blob around $0$ and must learn to split its output across all three peaks without ever seeing a single real training example directly — only the detective's feedback.

Watch the detective curve $D(x)$ in **Balanced duel**. Early on, $D(x)$ shoots near $1$ over the empty real peaks at $\pm 2.4$ and dips near $0$ where the forger piles too many fakes at $0$. That slope in $D(x)$ literally pushes the forger's samples downhill and uphill toward the missing peaks. By step 240, all three peaks are covered (`30% · 19% · 33%`, distribution error $W_1 = 0.307$) and $D(x)$ flattens toward $0.5$ — the theoretical equilibrium where even the best detective can do no better than flipping a fair coin. <Src href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661" />

## Two ways the duel breaks

Unlike a supervised classifier rolling down a single convex bowl, a GAN is two optimisers chasing each other across a moving saddle surface. Two failure modes haunt almost every GAN run — and you can trigger both right in the widget above.

### 1. When the detective gets too good

<Predict
  question="Suppose the detective gets far ahead of the forger early on, scoring every fake near D(G(z)) ≈ 0. Under the original minimax generator loss log(1 − D(G(z))), what happens to the forger's learning signal?"
  options={[
    "It vanishes: log(1 − p) is nearly flat near p = 0, so the forger gets almost no gradient to improve",
    "It becomes huge, giving the forger an instant roadmap to the real peaks",
    "Nothing changes, because the generator does not use D's derivative"
  ]}
  answer={0}
>
It vanishes. When $D$ ends in a sigmoid $p = \sigma(u)$, the derivative of $\log(1 - p)$ with respect to the logit $u$ is simply $-p$. When $D$ spots 99.8% of fakes ($p = 0.0021$ in our **Detective too strong** preset), that derivative shrinks to almost zero: the forger's raw gradient norm collapses to **0.0122**.

Goodfellow and colleagues spotted this in the very first GAN paper and suggested a one-line fix: instead of minimising $\log(1 - D(G(z)))$, train $G$ to minimise the **non-saturating loss** $-\log D(G(z))$. Its derivative with respect to the logit is $p - 1$, which approaches $-1$ when $p \to 0$. On the exact same frozen networks in our preset, switching to $-\log D$ raises the forger's gradient norm from **0.0122** to **5.483** — **449× stronger**! (An honest note on optimisers: Adam rescales small gradients by their running RMS, which hides mild vanishing until floating-point underflow; plain SGD exposes the stall immediately.)
</Predict>

### 2. Mode collapse

Now switch the widget above to **Mode collapse** and jump to step 240. Every sample the forger generates looks cleanly like real data — 100% of them sit squarely inside the middle peak at $0$! Yet the left peak ($-2.4$) and right peak ($+2.4$) get **0%** of the samples ($W_1 = 1.462$).

That is **<Term id="mode-collapse">mode collapse</Term>**: because the detective inspects one sample at a time, a forger that learns to make one flawless banknote earns high marks on every bill it prints — even though it has forgotten every other denomination. The samples look crisp, yet diversity has collapsed.

## Writing the minimax duel down

Put the detective's objective (recognise real data $x \sim p_{\text{data}}$ as $1$ and fakes $G(z)$ as $0$) together with the forger's opposite goal in a single value function:

<Eq
  words="\text{forger minimises, detective maximises: } \text{average } \log D(\text{real}) + \text{average } \log(1 - D(\text{fake}))"
  tex="\min_{\cb{G}} \max_{\ca{D}}\; \cc{V}(\ca{D}, \cb{G}) = \mathbb{E}_{\cg{x} \sim p_{\text{data}}}\!\big[\log \ca{D}(\cg{x})\big] + \mathbb{E}_{\cp{z} \sim p_z}\!\big[\log\!\big(1 - \ca{D}(\cb{G}(\cp{z}))\big)\big]"
>

- $\ca{D}(\cdot)$ — the **discriminator** (detective), outputting a probability in $(0, 1)$ that its input is real
- $\cb{G}(\cdot)$ — the **generator** (forger), turning random noise into synthetic data
- $\cg{x} \sim p_{\text{data}}$ — a real example drawn from the training distribution
- $\cp{z} \sim p_z$ — a random noise vector (here 1D or 2D standard normal) giving the forger variety
- $\cc{V}(\ca{D}, \cb{G})$ — the **minimax payoff**: $\ca{D}$ climbs to make $\ca{D}(\cg{x}) \to 1$ and $\ca{D}(\cb{G}(\cp{z})) \to 0$; $\cb{G}$ descends to make $\ca{D}(\cb{G}(\cp{z})) \to 1$ (in practice using the non-saturating $-\log \ca{D}(\cb{G}(\cp{z}))$ loss)

</Eq>

## Lab: Keep the Duel Balanced

Keeping two adversarial networks in step across random initialisations is the real craft of GAN training. In this lab you control three levers — the **learning-rate ratio** $\text{lr}_G / \text{lr}_D$, **discriminator steps per generator step**, and **noise dimension** (1D or 2D) — and every configuration is scored fairly across **five fixed seeds**.

<Deeper title="Debrief: what the 30-setting, 5-seed search found" tag="Spoiler">

Searching all 30 combinations (5 LR ratios × 3 D-steps × 2 noise dimensions, each averaged over the 5 fixed seeds for 240 outer steps, base $\text{lr}_D = 0.015$) reveals a striking pattern:

- **Brief 1 (average ≥ 2.0 modes and $W_1 \le 0.80$):** **19 of 30** settings pass.
- **Brief 2 (average ≥ 2.8 modes and $W_1 \le 0.45$):** **6 of 30** settings pass — and **every single passing setting runs the discriminator faster than the generator** ($\text{lr}_G / \text{lr}_D \in \{0.25\times, 0.5\times\}$), matching the real-world Two Time-Scale Update Rule (TTUR) used to stabilise large GANs.
- **Both extremes fail.** Pushing the generator faster than the discriminator ($\text{lr}_G / \text{lr}_D = 4\times$) collapses or diverges (`0.4`–`2.0` modes, $W_1 > 1.0$). Yet pushing the discriminator *too* hard (`4 D-steps` paired with `0.25×` LR ratio) **also** collapses (`1.4`–`1.6` modes, `0/5` seeds covering all three).
- **Brief 3 (fewest total updates, best found by searching the 30-config grid):** **`lrRatio = 0.5×, D-steps = 1, noiseDim = 2`** reaches the 3-mode quality bar in **40 total updates** (20 outer steps × 2 updates/step) and finishes at **3.0/3 modes across all 5 seeds** ($W_1 = 0.396$). By comparison, `lrRatio = 0.25×, D-steps = 2, noiseDim = 2` achieves the lowest final error ($W_1 = 0.272$), but paying two discriminator steps per iteration triples the update budget to hit the bar (120 updates).

</Deeper>

<ForReal>
Real GAN training pipelines (DCGAN, StyleGAN, BigGAN) use the exact mechanics you just tuned: Adam with lowered momentum $\beta_1 = 0.5$ (so fast-changing adversarial gradients don't overshoot), separate learning rates for $G$ and $D$ (TTUR), and non-saturating $-\log D(G(z))$ or hinge / Wasserstein losses. In PyTorch, the single most common bug is forgetting `.detach()` on `G(z)` during the discriminator step — which wastes memory and corrupts gradients when you only wanted to update $D$.
</ForReal>

## In code

Here is the core alternating GAN loop in thirty lines of pure NumPy — a linear generator learning to match a real Gaussian at mean `2.00`, std `0.40` (starting far away at `-1.50`), with non-saturating backprop through a frozen discriminator:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
sigmoid = lambda x: 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-np.clip(x, -30, 30)))

w_g, b_g = 0.7, -1.5   # starts far from the real peak at +2.0
W1 = rng.normal(0, 0.7, (2, 12)); b1 = np.zeros(12)
W2 = rng.normal(0, 0.5, (12, 1)); b2 = np.zeros(1)

for step in range(800):
    xr = rng.normal(2.0, 0.4, (48, 1))
    z = rng.normal(0, 1.0, (48, 1))
    xf = w_g * z + b_g
    x_all = np.vstack([xr, xf])
    X = np.hstack([x_all, x_all ** 2])
    y = np.vstack([np.ones((48, 1)), np.zeros((48, 1))])
    h = np.tanh(X @ W1 + b1)
    p = sigmoid(h @ W2 + b2)
    dz2 = (p - y) / len(X)
    dh = (dz2 @ W2.T) * (1 - h * h)
    W2 -= 0.08 * (h.T @ dz2); b2 -= 0.08 * dz2.sum(0)
    W1 -= 0.08 * (X.T @ dh);  b1 -= 0.08 * dh.sum(0)

    z = rng.normal(0, 1.0, (48, 1))
    xf = w_g * z + b_g
    Xf = np.hstack([xf, xf ** 2])
    h = np.tanh(Xf @ W1 + b1)
    p = sigmoid(h @ W2 + b2)
    dz2 = (p - 1.0) / len(z)        # non-saturating: d(-log sigma(u))/du = p - 1
    dXf = ((dz2 @ W2.T) * (1 - h * h)) @ W1.T
    dxf = dXf[:, 0:1] + 2.0 * xf * dXf[:, 1:2]
    w_g -= 0.015 * float((dxf * z).sum())
    b_g -= 0.015 * float(dxf.sum())

fakes = w_g * rng.normal(0, 1.0, 2000) + b_g
print(f"fake mean = {fakes.mean():.2f} (real 2.00), fake std = {fakes.std():.2f} (real 0.40)")
```

```
fake mean = 2.03 (real 2.00), fake std = 0.48 (real 0.40)
```

<Misconception>

"A falling generator loss means your GAN is getting better." Not in a minimax game! Because the detective changes at every step, a dropping generator loss can mean the discriminator just got temporarily worse, and a rising generator loss often happens *while* generated samples are steadily sharpening. That is why GAN work tracks distribution metrics (like our 1D Wasserstein distance $W_1$, or Fréchet Inception Distance on images) alongside visual inspection, never raw loss curves alone.

</Misconception>

<Takeaways>

- A **GAN** replaces blurry pixel-error reconstruction with a duel between a **generator** $G(z)$ and a **discriminator** $D(x)$ in a minimax game whose global equilibrium is $p_G = p_{\text{data}}$ and $D(x) = 0.5$ everywhere.
- Under the original $\log(1 - D(G(z)))$ loss, an over-confident detective ($D(\text{fake}) \approx 0$) starves the forger of gradient; the **non-saturating** $-\log D(G(z))$ loss preserves a strong gradient (449× larger in our preset).
- **Mode collapse** happens when the generator produces sharp, realistic samples from only a subset of the data's modes while ignoring the rest.
- Keeping the duel balanced requires careful pacing: in our 30-setting, 5-seed search, running $D$ slightly faster than $G$ ($\text{lr}_G / \text{lr}_D = 0.5\times$, 1 D-step, 2D noise) covered all 3 modes in 40 updates, while both overly fast $G$ and overly dominant $D$ collapsed.

</Takeaways>

<InTheWild>

From 2015 to 2020, GANs led photorealistic image synthesis — from DCGAN and pix2pix image-to-image translation to StyleGAN's faces and super-resolution upscalers — and adversarial discriminator losses still survive inside modern video/image VAE decoders (like VQGAN and Stable Diffusion's `AutoencoderKL` training) to sharpen fine texture. Yet training instability and mode collapse remained persistent headaches, and in May 2021 Dhariwal and Nichol showed that **diffusion models** surpassed BigGAN-deep on ImageNet synthesis with better distribution coverage. <Src href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.05233" />

</InTheWild>

GANs generate sharp samples in a single forward pass, at the cost of balancing a delicate two-player duel. What if you traded one-shot speed for rock-solid supervised training — dissolving data into noise step by step and training one network to run the film backwards? That is <LessonLink to="diffusion">diffusion</LessonLink>.
