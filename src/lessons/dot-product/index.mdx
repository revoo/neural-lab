## The problem: one number from two lists

In <LessonLink to="vectors">the vectors lesson</LessonLink> we turned emails into vectors. That's step one. Step two is to *do* something with them — and very often, what we want is a single number.

How spammy is this email? How relevant is this document to my search? How much should this word pay attention to that word? Each question takes two vectors in and needs one score out.

The operation that does this — the **dot product** — is almost embarrassingly simple. It is also, without much exaggeration, the most important operation in deep learning. Every artificial neuron computes one. Every attention head in every transformer computes millions of them. Every "find similar documents" feature runs on them.

So let's get to know it properly.

<MachineMap highlight="weighted-sum" />

**Where this is going.** A neuron's first step is to weigh each piece of evidence and add it up. That step is a dot product, and so is the "how relevant is this word?" score at the heart of every transformer.

## You already know how to do this

<Analogy title="Think of a shopping receipt">

You buy 2 apples at £0.50, 1 loaf of bread at £2.20 and 3 cartons of milk at £1.20. What's the total?

You multiply each quantity by its price and add everything up: $2 \times 0.50 + 1 \times 2.20 + 3 \times 1.20 = 6.80$.

Congratulations: you've just computed a dot product. The quantities form one vector, `[2, 1, 3]`. The prices form another, `[0.50, 2.20, 1.20]`. The dot product pairs up matching entries, multiplies each pair, and sums the results.

</Analogy>

That's the whole definition. Here it is as a live receipt, with the loop exposed so you can watch it run.

<DotTable />

Written in the usual shorthand, it looks like this — and it says exactly what the receipt does:

<Eq
  words="\text{total} = \text{add up, over every item, } (\text{quantity} \times \text{price})"
  tex="\ca{\mathbf{a}} \cdot \cb{\mathbf{b}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ca{a_i}\, \cb{b_i} = \ca{a_1}\cb{b_1} + \ca{a_2}\cb{b_2} + \cdots + \ca{a_n}\cb{b_n}"
>

- $\ca{a_i}$ — entry $i$ of the first vector (the **quantity** of item $i$)
- $\cb{b_i}$ — entry $i$ of the second vector (the **price** of item $i$)
- $\sum_{i=1}^{n}$ — the `for` loop in the code panel; $\ca{a_i}\cb{b_i}$ is the line inside it

</Eq>

### From receipts to judgements

Now change what the second vector means. Instead of *prices*, make it *how much you care about each feature*.

Say you're writing a crude spam detector by hand. Emails are `[links, exclamation marks, times your name appears]`. You decide each link is mildly suspicious (+1), each exclamation mark more so (+2), and an email that addresses you by name is probably legitimate (−3). Your opinion is a vector of **<Term id="weight">weights</Term>**: `[1, 2, -3]`.

An email with 4 links, 5 exclamation marks and no mention of your name scores

$$
[4, 5, 0] \cdot [1, 2, -3] = 4 + 10 + 0 = 14
$$

— fairly spammy. A message with 1 link, 1 exclamation mark and your name twice scores $1 + 2 - 6 = -3$: probably fine.

The calculation is identical to the receipt. All that changed is the story. This is exactly what the first half of an artificial neuron does, as you'll see in <LessonLink to="neuron">a few lessons</LessonLink>. Positive weights say "this feature is evidence *for*". Negative weights say "evidence *against*". The dot product tallies up the evidence.

## The geometric secret

So far, the dot product is bookkeeping. But remember that vectors live a double life: they're also arrows. What does multiply-and-add mean for arrows?

Here's the surprising answer: **the dot product measures how much two arrows point in the same direction.**

Play with it before reading on. Drag the two vectors around and watch the sign and size of the dot product.

<Predict
  question="Two weight arrows point in exactly perpendicular directions. What is their dot product?"
  options={["0", "1", "It depends on their lengths"]}
  answer={0}
>

Perpendicular means no agreement at all: each arrow's shadow on the other has length zero, whatever their lengths. Set the angle to 90° in the explorer and check.

</Predict>

<DotExplorer />

Here is what you should have found:

- When the arrows point roughly the **same way**, the dot product is **positive**.
- When they're **perpendicular**, it's **exactly zero**, no matter how long they are.
- When they point in **opposing directions**, it's **negative**.
- For fixed lengths, it's **largest when they point in exactly the same direction**.

Look at the shading. The dashed line through the origin divides the plane into a blue half and an orange half. Every possible $\mathbf{b}$ in the blue half has a positive dot product with $\mathbf{a}$; every one in the orange half is negative. So the dot product with a fixed vector **splits space in two with a straight line**. File that away: it is exactly how a single neuron makes a decision.

### Why would multiply-and-add measure direction?

It feels like a coincidence that receipt arithmetic should know anything about angles. It isn't. Look at each product $a_i b_i$ on its own:

- If $a_i$ and $b_i$ have the **same sign** — both vectors lean the same way along that axis — the product is positive and pushes the total up.
- If they have **opposite signs**, the product is negative and drags the total down.
- If either is near zero, that axis contributes almost nothing.

So the dot product is a vote, held separately along every axis, on whether the two vectors agree. Large entries get a bigger vote. When the vectors point the same way, every axis agrees and the votes pile up. When they're perpendicular, the agreeing and disagreeing votes cancel exactly.

### The same idea, seen as a shadow

There's a second way to see this that makes the geometry obvious — and once you have it, the formula writes itself.

<Analogy title="Think of a shadow">

Lay $\mathbf{a}$ down as a line on the floor. Hold a torch directly above and look at the shadow that $\mathbf{b}$ casts onto that line — the green bar in the explorer.

Ask yourself: *how much of $\mathbf{b}$ is going in $\mathbf{a}$'s direction?* That's exactly what the shadow measures. If $\mathbf{b}$ points along $\mathbf{a}$, the shadow is all of $\mathbf{b}$. If $\mathbf{b}$ stands perpendicular, the shadow shrinks to a point. If $\mathbf{b}$ leans backwards, the shadow falls *behind* the origin, and we count it as negative.

</Analogy>

Now build the dot product out of that picture, one piece at a time:

1. **How long is the shadow?** It depends on how long $\mathbf{b}$ is, and on how much it tilts away from $\mathbf{a}$. Write the tilt as an angle $\theta$ (theta). Tilting shrinks the shadow by some factor between 1 (no tilt) and 0 (perpendicular), going negative if you tilt past 90°. Trigonometry has a name for exactly that shrink factor: $\cos\theta$. So the shadow is $\|\mathbf{b}\| \cos\theta$.
2. **Then scale by $\mathbf{a}$.** The dot product also grows with the length of $\mathbf{a}$ — double $\mathbf{a}$ and every product $a_i b_i$ doubles. So multiply by $\|\mathbf{a}\|$.

Put those two sentences side by side with the formula and you'll see it's just a transcription:

$$
\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \underbrace{\|\mathbf{a}\|}_{\text{length of } \mathbf{a}} \; \times \; \underbrace{\|\mathbf{b}\| \cos\theta}_{\text{shadow of } \mathbf{b} \text{ on } \mathbf{a}}
$$

If trigonometry is rusty, $\cos\theta$ is all you need from it, and you can read it as "how aligned": **1** at 0° (same direction), **0** at 90° (perpendicular), **−1** at 180° (opposite), sliding smoothly in between. That's precisely the pattern you found by dragging the arrows.

The explorer computes this version and the multiply-and-add version separately. Check that they always agree: they're two descriptions of one quantity. One is convenient for computers (no angles needed, just a loop). The other is convenient for understanding — it splits the dot product into *how long are the vectors?* and *how aligned are they?*

<Deeper title="Why do the two formulas agree?" tag="Maths">

Here's an argument that avoids heavy trigonometry. Two facts do all the work.

**Fact 1: rotating both vectors together doesn't change the dot product.** The angle between them is unchanged, and so are their lengths; less obviously, the sum $a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2$ is unchanged too (you can verify this in the explorer by rotating both arrows by the same amount — the number stays put). The dot product only cares about the *relationship* between the vectors, not how they're oriented on the page.

**Fact 2: so rotate until $\mathbf{a}$ lies flat along the x-axis.** Now $\mathbf{a} = [\|\mathbf{a}\|, 0]$. Its second entry is zero, so the dot product becomes just $\|\mathbf{a}\| \times b_1$. And $b_1$ — the horizontal component of $\mathbf{b}$ — is exactly the shadow $\mathbf{b}$ casts on the x-axis: $\|\mathbf{b}\|\cos\theta$.

Put them together: $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\|\,\|\mathbf{b}\|\cos\theta$. The same argument works in any number of dimensions.

</Deeper>

  question="You double every number in the email vector. What happens to its spam score $\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x}$?"
  options={["It doubles", "It stays the same", "It quadruples"]}
  answer={0}
>

The dot product is linear in each argument, so twice the evidence gives twice the score. That's why the cosine section that follows divides by length.

</Predict>

## Cosine similarity: direction only

The shadow picture reveals a catch. The dot product mixes up two different things: alignment and length. Sometimes you only care about one of them.

<Predict
  question="A search engine represents your query as $[1, 1]$. Document A is $[10, 1]$ and document B is $[2, 2]$. Which document has the **higher dot product** with the query?"
  options={['Document A', 'Document B', 'They tie']}
  answer={0}
>

Document A wins, $11$ to $4$ — but look at the directions. B points in *exactly* the same direction as the query: it's the same balance of topics, just a different overall size. A is overwhelmingly about the first topic, and wins only because it's big. If "big" just means "a longer document that repeats words more often", that's a bad reason to rank it first.

</Predict>

The fix is to divide out the lengths, leaving only the alignment:

<Eq
  words="\text{similarity of direction} = \frac{\text{dot product}}{\text{length of } \mathbf{a} \times \text{length of } \mathbf{b}}"
  tex="\text{cosine similarity} = \frac{\ca{\mathbf{a}}\cdot\cb{\mathbf{b}}}{\|\ca{\mathbf{a}}\|\,\|\cb{\mathbf{b}}\|} = \cos\theta"
>

- top: the **dot product** — alignment mixed with length
- bottom: the two **lengths** multiplied together — divide them out and only alignment remains
- $\cos\theta$ — the **alignment** itself: 1 same direction, 0 perpendicular, −1 opposite

</Eq>

It always lands between −1 and 1. For the search example, B now scores a perfect 1.0, and A scores about 0.77. Turn on the **unit vectors** view in the explorer to see this: shrinking both arrows to length 1 throws away their size and keeps only their direction.

Which one should you use? Neither is "correct". Use the raw dot product when magnitude carries meaning — a neuron's weights *should* respond more strongly to stronger evidence. Use cosine similarity when it doesn't — two documents about the same thing should match regardless of length. Many modern embedding models sidestep the question by normalising every vector to length 1 up front, at which point the dot product and cosine similarity become the same number.

<Deeper title="Debrief: what the sweeps found" tag="Spoiler">

**Brief 1.** Making $\mathbf{w}$ longer stretches every score by the same factor, so it never changes the order: only the *direction* of $\mathbf{w}$ matters. Sweeping that direction all the way round in steps of 0.01° shows one band that works, from 9.47° to 81.86°. A wide target, but a random direction still misses it about four times in five.

**Brief 2.** With $\mathbf{w}$ fixed at length 1, the gap between the lowest spam score and the highest real score peaks at **2.967**, with $\mathbf{w}$ pointing at 38.66°. Many nearby directions reach 95% of that.

**Brief 3.** On links and exclamation marks alone, no direction can separate the bank email from spam. Every one of the 5,944 directions (on a 1° grid over the sphere) that ranks perfectly puts a **negative** weight on mentions of your name. A dot product can count evidence *against* as easily as evidence for: a negative weight subtracts.

**Brief 4.** The long newsletter points exactly the same way as the short one but is twice as long, so its dot product doubles with it, and it overtakes the parcel spam for *every* direction of $\mathbf{w}$. Cosine similarity divides by length, so only direction is compared, and directions from 40.42° to 52.97° work again.

</Deeper>

<ForReal>

Early statistical spam filters were close cousins of this lab: count words, weigh the counts, add them up, and compare with a cut-off. The length cheat is real too. A long document mentions everything more often, which is why search engines compare word-count vectors by cosine rather than by raw dot product, and why embedding search today usually normalises every vector to length 1 first.

</ForReal>

## In code

```python
import numpy as np

a = np.array([3.0, 1.0])
b = np.array([1.5, 3.0])

np.dot(a, b)    # 7.5
a @ b           # 7.5   — the @ operator means "matrix/dot product" in NumPy and PyTorch
(a * b).sum()   # 7.5   — the definition, spelled out: element-wise multiply, then sum

def cosine_similarity(a, b):
    return (a @ b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))

cosine_similarity(a, b)  # 0.707...  → an angle of 45°
```

Notice `a * b` versus `a @ b`. The `*` operator multiplies element by element and gives you back a vector; `@` goes the extra step and sums. Mixing them up is a classic bug, and the error message (if you're lucky enough to get one) is usually about shapes.

<Misconception>

"A dot product of zero means the vectors have nothing to do with each other." That's true in one precise sense — neither has any component along the other's direction — but don't over-read it. The vectors $[1, 0]$ and $[0, 1]$ have a dot product of zero, yet one is just the other rotated by 90°. Zero dot product means *no linear alignment*, not *no relationship of any kind*. This distinction matters later, when people interpret similarity scores between embeddings as if they were measures of meaning.

</Misconception>

<Takeaways>

- The dot product multiplies matching entries and adds them up — a shopping receipt, or a tally of weighted evidence.
- Geometrically, it measures **alignment**: positive when vectors agree, zero when perpendicular, negative when they oppose.
- $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\|\|\mathbf{b}\|\cos\theta$: it blends length and direction. **Cosine similarity** divides out the lengths to keep only direction.
- Fixing one vector and asking "is the dot product positive?" splits space in half with a straight line. That's a decision rule — and it's the core of the artificial neuron.

</Takeaways>

<InTheWild>

The dot product is everywhere once you know to look. Each **neuron** in a neural network computes the dot product of its inputs with its weights. Inside a **transformer**, attention decides how much one token should listen to another by taking the dot product of a "query" vector with a "key" vector — billions of times per response. **Semantic search** and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) rank documents by the cosine similarity of their embedding vectors to the query's. And **recommendation systems** famously score how much you'll like a film as the dot product of a "you" vector with a "film" vector. GPUs are, in large part, machines built to do this one operation extremely fast.

</InTheWild>
