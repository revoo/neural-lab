# LoRA & Low-Rank Fine-Tuning

Suppose you have downloaded **Llama-3 8B** — 8 billion weights occupying **15 GiB** in BF16 — and you want to fine-tune it on your company’s support tickets or SQL dialect. You rent a shiny **80 GiB H100**, load the model, hit `train()`, and immediately crash with **`CUDA Out Of Memory`**.

Wait — how can a **15 GiB** model overflow an **80 GiB** GPU before you even feed in a batch of text?

Because *training* with mixed-precision <Term id="adam">AdamW</Term> does not store just the BF16 weights (2 bytes/param). For every trainable weight, it also keeps an FP32 master copy (4 bytes), a BF16 gradient (2 bytes), and Adam’s two FP32 moving averages $m$ and $v$ (8 bytes) — **16 bytes per trainable parameter**, or **119.7 GiB** for Llama-3 8B (and **1,052 GiB** for 70B!). Worse still, if you serve 50 customers, storing 50 separate full fine-tunes means 50 × 15.3 GB = **765 GB** of checkpoints!

**Low-Rank Adaptation (<Term id="lora">LoRA</Term>)** solves both problems with one linear-algebra observation: *when you adapt a pretrained model to a new task, the change you make to each weight matrix is tiny in rank, even when the matrix itself is huge.*

---

## 1. The low-rank hypothesis: factoring $\Delta W = B A$

When you fine-tune a $d \times d$ weight matrix $W_0$, the final weights are simply $W = W_0 + \Delta W$. Full fine-tuning treats all $d^2$ entries of $\Delta W$ as completely independent numbers — for a single $4,096 \times 4,096$ Llama-3 layer, that is **16.8 million** trainable numbers in one matrix alone.

But what if the task shift $\Delta W$ really only pushes inputs along $r = 16$ directions instead of all $4,096$? Then instead of storing the whole $d \times d$ update $\Delta W$, we freeze $W_0$ completely and factor $\Delta W$ into two skinny rectangles — a **down-projection** $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d_{\text{in}}}$ and an **up-projection** $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times r}$:

Count the trainable parameters on a $4,096 \times 4,096$ matrix at $r = 16$:
- **Full matrix $\Delta W$:** $4,096 \times 4,096 = 16,777,216$ parameters.
- **LoRA ($A$ and $B$):** $16 \times (4,096 + 4,096) = 131,072$ parameters — **128× fewer!**

And crucially, **LoRA only works because real fine-tuning updates have low intrinsic rank.** Test the difference yourself on two 12×12 matrices below: a structured task shift vs pure random noise.

Notice the sanity check when you drag $r$ past 6 on a 12×12 matrix: $24r$ actually *exceeds* $144$! LoRA only compresses a matrix when $r$ is well below $\tfrac{1}{2}\min(d_{\text{in}}, d_{\text{out}})$ — which is wildly true in LLMs ($r = 16$ vs $d = 4,096$).

## 2. Where the VRAM actually goes — LoRA vs QLoRA

Now plug that parameter reduction into the mixed-precision AdamW memory budget. Because $W_0$ is **frozen**, we store zero master weights, zero gradients, and zero Adam $m, v$ states for 99.5% of the network!

On **Llama-3 8B** (with $r = 16$ applied to all linear projections — attention $Q, K, V, O$ plus MLP Gate, Up, Down):
- **Full fine-tuning:** 8.03B trainable params → **119.7 GiB** total (**59.8 GiB** in Adam states alone!) and a **15.3 GiB** checkpoint per task.
- **LoRA (BF16 base):** **41.9M** trainable params (**0.52%**) → **15.6 GiB** total (**0.31 GiB** Adam states — **193× smaller!**) and an **80 MiB** adapter file.
- **<Term id="qlora">QLoRA</Term> (4-bit base):** store the frozen $W_0$ in 4-bit (from [Floating-Point Formats & Quantisation](#/learn/quantization)) while keeping tiny BF16 adapters → **4.8 GiB** total on 8B, and just **40.1 GiB** on **Llama-3 70B** (down from **1,052 GiB** — a **26×** reduction that puts 70B fine-tuning on a single GPU!).

Once training finishes, matrix addition is linear:

$$W_{\text{merged}} \;=\; W_0 \;+\; \frac{\alpha}{r}\, B A.$$

You add $\frac{\alpha}{r}BA$ directly into $W_0$ **offline** before deployment, producing an ordinary weight matrix with **zero** extra inference FLOPs and **zero** extra latency. Alternatively, if you serve 100 different customer fine-tunes from one GPU, you keep one shared frozen $W_0$ in VRAM and store 100 tiny 80 MiB `(A, B)` adapters alongside it — dynamically applying each customer’s adapter on the fly!

## 3. The zero-initialisation puzzle: why $B = 0$ and $A \sim \mathcal{N}$?

Here is a subtle detail that trips up almost everyone implementing LoRA from scratch. At step 0, we want the adapter to add **zero** to the pretrained model ($\Delta W_0 = BA = 0$) so training starts smoothly from the pretrained weights without scrambling them.

Notice an honest quirk on our tiny 8×8 linear problem: **Both random** spikes step-0 loss from `0.541` up to **`0.866`**, yet still recovers cleanly over 80 steps because a single linear layer has a convex quadratic landscape. On a real 32-layer pretrained Transformer, corrupting every attention and MLP matrix with random noise at step 0 destroys the delicate internal representations learned over trillions of tokens — which is why **`B = 0, A ~ N`** is non-negotiable in practice.

## 4. Lab: Spend your LoRA rank budget where the shift lives

Standard LoRA assigns the exact same rank $r$ to every layer. Yet in real fine-tuning, early layers often need almost no change (basic syntax already transfers), while middle reasoning layers and output heads carry most of the task shift — the insight behind **AdaLoRA** and rank-allocation methods.

## 5. Ten lines of Python for exact LoRA & QLoRA VRAM budgets

You can verify our exact Llama-3 8B and 70B parameter counts and fine-tuning VRAM footprints in ten lines of plain Python:

```python
def lora_summary(layers, d_model, d_kv, d_ffn, params_b, rank=16):
    per_layer = rank * (2 * (d_model + d_model) + 2 * (d_kv + d_model) + 3 * (d_ffn + d_model))
    trainable = layers * per_layer
    gib = 1024 ** 3
    full_gib = (params_b * 1e9 * 16) / gib
    lora_gib = (params_b * 1e9 * 2 + trainable * 16) / gib
    qlora_gib = (params_b * 1e9 * 0.5625 + trainable * 16) / gib
    print(f"{params_b}B r={rank}: trainable={trainable/1e6:.1f}M ({trainable/(params_b*1e9)*100:.2f}%) | Full={full_gib:.1f} GiB | LoRA={lora_gib:.1f} GiB | QLoRA={qlora_gib:.1f} GiB")

lora_summary(32, 4096, 1024, 14336, 8.03, rank=16)
lora_summary(80, 8192, 1024, 28672, 70.6, rank=16)
```

Running it prints:

```text
8.03B r=16: trainable=41.9M (0.52%) | Full=119.7 GiB | LoRA=15.6 GiB | QLoRA=4.8 GiB
70.6B r=16: trainable=207.1M (0.29%) | Full=1052.0 GiB | LoRA=134.6 GiB | QLoRA=40.1 GiB
```
