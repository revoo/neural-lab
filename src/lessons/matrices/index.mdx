## The problem: vectors in, vectors out

The dot product takes two vectors and produces a single number. That's great for scoring, but a neural network needs something more ambitious.

Picture the first layer of an image classifier. In goes a vector of 784 pixel values. Out comes a vector of, say, 128 numbers, each one describing some pattern in the image. The layer is a machine that turns **one vector into another vector** — often of a different size.

We need a mathematical machine for that. The simplest useful one is a **<Term id="matrix">matrix</Term>**: a rectangular grid of numbers. This lesson is about how to *read* a matrix — not as a table of data, but as a description of motion.

<MachineMap highlight="layer" />

**Where this is going.** One neuron is one dot product. A layer of neurons is many dot products at once, and writing them as a single matrix is what lets a GPU compute a whole layer in one go.

## First reading: a panel of judges

You already know half of the story. In <LessonLink to="dot-product">the dot product lesson</LessonLink>, one vector of weights scored an email with a dot product. What if you want *several* scores at once — say, how spammy, how urgent and how personal?

Hire a panel of judges. Each judge has their own weight vector, and each produces their own dot product. Stack the judges' weight vectors as the rows of a grid, and you have a matrix:

$$
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & -3 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \\ -1 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{three judges}}
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{email}}
=
\begin{bmatrix} 1\cdot4 + 2\cdot5 + (-3)\cdot0 \\ 0\cdot4 + 1\cdot5 + 4\cdot0 \\ (-1)\cdot4 + 0\cdot5 + 2\cdot0 \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} 14 \\ 5 \\ -4 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Each output entry is one row dotted with the input. That's it: **multiplying a matrix by a vector is a batch of dot products**, one per row. If you can compute dot products, you can compute this.

This "rows view" is how a neural network layer is usually *computed*. But it doesn't tell you much about what the matrix *does*. For that, we need the second reading.

## Second reading: a machine that moves space

Shrink things down to two dimensions so we can watch. A 2×2 matrix takes any 2D point and moves it to a new 2D point. Apply it to *every* point on the plane at once and you get a transformation of the entire space: stretching, rotating, shearing, flipping.

If you've ever written `transform: matrix(...)` in CSS, or rotated a sprite in a game engine, you've used exactly this idea.

<Analogy title="Think of a recipe card for moving space">

Suppose someone hands you an instruction: "here's how to move every point on the page." It might seem like you'd need an infinite list — one entry per point. You don't. You only need two facts:

1. Where does the arrow $[1, 0]$ end up?
2. Where does the arrow $[0, 1]$ end up?

Every other point is built out of those two. The point $[3, 2]$ means "three steps of $[1, 0]$ plus two steps of $[0, 1]$". After the transformation, it's still three steps of wherever $[1, 0]$ went, plus two steps of wherever $[0, 1]$ went.

A 2×2 matrix is that recipe card. **Its first column is where $[1, 0]$ lands. Its second column is where $[0, 1]$ lands.** Four numbers describe the motion of the entire infinite plane.

</Analogy>

Those two special arrows have names: the **<Term id="basis-vectors">basis vectors</Term>** $\hat{\imath}$ ("i-hat", pointing right) and $\hat{\jmath}$ ("j-hat", pointing up). Try it: grab the tips of $\hat{\imath}$ and $\hat{\jmath}$ below and drag them around. You're editing the matrix's columns directly, and the whole grid follows.

<GridTransformer />

Some things to try, and what to notice:

- **Press "Rotate 30°"** and look at the matrix. The columns are the rotated $\hat{\imath}$ and $\hat{\jmath}$, and the numbers are whatever cosines and sines that rotation needs. You don't have to memorise the rotation formula — you can *derive* it by asking where the two basis vectors go.
- **Watch the blue vector $M\mathbf{v}$.** The dashed path shows it being built as "$v_x$ steps of the new $\hat{\imath}$, then $v_y$ steps of the new $\hat{\jmath}$". That's the columns view of matrix-vector multiplication, drawn literally.
- **Whatever you do, grid lines stay straight, parallel, and evenly spaced, and the origin never moves.** That's the defining property of a **<Term id="linear-transformation">linear transformation</Term>**, and it's why matrices are so predictable.
- **Try "Squash flat".** The whole plane collapses onto a single line. Points that were different now land on top of each other. That information is gone for good — no matrix can un-squash it.

## Writing it down

Here's the general 2×2 case, with the columns coloured to match the explorer: the green column is where $\hat{\imath}$ lands, the red one is where $\hat{\jmath}$ lands.

<Eq
  words="\text{output} = (\text{steps right}) \times (\text{where } \hat{\imath} \text{ lands}) + (\text{steps up}) \times (\text{where } \hat{\jmath} \text{ lands})"
  tex="\begin{bmatrix} \cc{a} & \cv{b} \\ \cc{c} & \cv{d} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \cc{a}x + \cv{b}y \\ \cc{c}x + \cv{d}y \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{rows view: two dot products}} = \underbrace{x\begin{bmatrix} \cc{a} \\ \cc{c} \end{bmatrix} + y\begin{bmatrix} \cv{b} \\ \cv{d} \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{columns view: a blend of the columns}}"
>

- $x, y$ — the **input**: how far right and how far up the point starts
- $\cc{a}, \cc{c}$ — the **first column**: where the arrow $[1, 0]$ ends up
- $\cv{b}, \cv{d}$ — the **second column**: where the arrow $[0, 1]$ ends up

</Eq>

These are the same arithmetic, grouped differently. The rows view says *each output is a weighted sum of the inputs*. The columns view says *the output is a weighted blend of the columns, using the input as the weights*. Engineers tend to compute with the first and reason with the second.

A convention you'll meet constantly: an $m \times n$ matrix has $m$ rows and $n$ columns. It takes vectors with $n$ entries (one per column) and produces vectors with $m$ entries (one per row). So a layer turning 784 pixels into 128 features is a $128 \times 784$ matrix. Rows first, then columns — like `matrix[row][col]` in code.

### The determinant: how much does area change?

Watch the shaded square in the explorer. It starts as the 1×1 square sitting on $\hat{\imath}$ and $\hat{\jmath}$, and gets carried along by the transformation. Its new area is the **<Term id="determinant">determinant</Term>** of the matrix:

<Eq
  words="\text{area scaling factor} = (\text{top-left} \times \text{bottom-right}) - (\text{top-right} \times \text{bottom-left})"
  tex="\det M = \cc{a}\cv{d} - \cv{b}\cc{c}"
/>

Because a linear transformation treats every part of space the same way, *every* shape's area gets multiplied by this same number. A determinant of 2 doubles all areas; 0.5 halves them. A negative determinant means space has been flipped over, like turning a page face-down (the square turns orange). And a determinant of **0** means space has been squashed into a lower dimension — the alarm bell for lost information.

<Deeper title="Why can't a matrix move the origin?" tag="Edge case">

Multiply any matrix by the zero vector and every dot product is zero, so $M\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$. The origin is pinned in place no matter what.

That means a plain matrix can never "shift everything one unit to the right". A shift is not a linear transformation. Neural networks fix this by adding a separate vector after the multiplication — the **bias** — so each layer computes $M\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{b}$. That combination (a linear map followed by a shift) is called an *affine* transformation. You'll meet the bias properly in <LessonLink to="neuron">the neuron lesson</LessonLink>.

Graphics programmers solve the same problem differently: they add an extra dimension and use 3×3 matrices for 2D (or 4×4 for 3D), a trick called homogeneous coordinates. That's why CSS's `matrix()` takes six numbers rather than four.

</Deeper>

## In code

```python
import numpy as np

M = np.array([[1.0, 1.0],     # a 2×2 shear
              [0.0, 1.0]])
v = np.array([1.5, 1.0])

M @ v                 # array([2.5, 1. ])   matrix times vector
M[:, 0]               # array([1., 0.])     first column: where î lands
M[:, 1]               # array([1., 1.])     second column: where ĵ lands
np.linalg.det(M)      # 1.0                 shear preserves area

# The same thing as a batch of dot products, one per row:
np.array([row @ v for row in M])      # array([2.5, 1. ])
```

In PyTorch, a layer like `torch.nn.Linear(784, 128)` stores exactly such a matrix — 128 rows, 784 columns — plus a bias vector. Calling the layer computes `W @ x + b`.

<Misconception>

"A neural network layer is a big lookup table." It's natural to imagine each weight as a little fact the network memorised. A better picture is the one from the explorer: a layer's matrix **reshapes the whole space** of inputs at once — stretching some directions, compressing others, mixing features together. Learning means gradually adjusting that reshaping so that, by the last layer, the things you want to distinguish have been pulled apart.

</Misconception>

<Takeaways>

- A matrix times a vector is **a batch of dot products** — one per row. That's how it's computed.
- Equivalently, it's **a blend of the matrix's columns**, weighted by the input's entries. That's how to understand it.
- A matrix describes a **linear transformation** of space: lines stay straight and evenly spaced, and the origin stays fixed. Its columns record where the basis vectors land.
- The **determinant** tells you how areas scale. Zero means space is squashed flat and information is lost.
- An $m \times n$ matrix turns $n$-dimensional vectors into $m$-dimensional ones.

</Takeaways>

<InTheWild>

Almost all of the parameters in a modern neural network live in matrices. A single transformer layer in an 8-billion-parameter language model contains several weight matrices of shape roughly 4,096 × 4,096 and 4,096 × 14,336 — tens of millions of numbers each, describing how to reshape a 4,096-dimensional space. When people talk about a model's "weights", they mostly mean these matrices. And when GPU makers advertise performance, they're mostly advertising how fast their chips can multiply them.

</InTheWild>

<Bridge to="forward-propagation">

A layer computes $\cp{\mathbf{a}^{(l)}} = \cg{f}\big(\ca{W^{(l)}}\,\mathbf{a}^{(l-1)} + \cc{\mathbf{b}^{(l)}}\big)$: each row of $\ca{W}$ is one neuron's weight vector, the "panel of judges" from this lesson. Without the bend $\cg{f}$, any stack of these is still one matrix, which is why <LessonLink to="activation-functions">activation functions</LessonLink> exist.

</Bridge>

So far, one matrix acts on one vector. In practice, we push *many* vectors through *many* matrices, one after another. That's where matrix multiplication — and the shape rules that keep it straight — come in.
