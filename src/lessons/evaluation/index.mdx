## The problem: "Our new prompt jumped from 82% to 88%!"

You changed the system prompt of our Kestrel K2 support agent, ran it on 50 support tickets, and accuracy jumped from 82% to 88%. Six full percentage points! Time to ship?

Before you merge the pull request, ask three uncomfortable questions:

1. **Could that +6 points just be noise?** In the statistics lesson you saw that a test score wobbles like $\sqrt{p(1 - p)/n}$ — on 50 examples around 85%, one standard error is already 5 percentage points.
2. **How many prompts did you try before you saw 88%?** If you tweaked the prompt twenty times on those same 50 questions and kept the best one, you almost certainly overfitted your eval set.
3. **Who graded the answers?** If an LLM judge scored free-text responses, did it prefer the new prompt because it was genuinely better — or because it sat in Slot 1, or wrote twice as many words?

In AI systems engineering, **evaluation is the feedback loop**. A broken eval doesn't merely waste time; it actively guides your team in the wrong direction while displaying green charts the whole way. This lesson makes all three traps exact and measurable.

## Paired comparisons: why the *same* test set is worth 3× the data

Suppose Model A scores **84%** and Model B scores **78%** on 100 questions. If you evaluated Model A on one random sample of 100 questions and Model B on a *different* 100 questions, how surprising is a 6-point gap? Not surprising at all: two identical 81% models on separate 100-question samples produce a gap of 6 points or more **32.1%** of the time ($p = 0.321$). You learned nothing.

Now run **both** models on the **exact same 100 questions** — a **<Term id="paired-eval">paired evaluation</Term>**. Every question falls into one of four boxes: both right ($n_{11}$), both wrong ($n_{00}$), only A right ($n_{10}$), or only B right ($n_{01}$).

Think about the questions where both models agreed. Easy questions ("What's the warranty?") get answered right by both; impossible questions get missed by both. Neither tells you which model is better. **Only the disagreements carry signal.** If A and B are truly equally good, each disagreement is a fair coin flip — 50% chance A wins, 50% chance B wins. Checking how lopsided the coin flips are is **McNemar's exact test**:

<Eq
  words="\cc{\text{chance of seeing a split this lopsided by luck}} = 2 \sum_{k = \max(\ca{n_{10}}, \cb{n_{01}})}^{\cp{m}} \binom{\cp{m}}{k} \Big(\frac{1}{2}\Big)^{\cp{m}}"
  tex="\cc{p_{\text{McNemar}}} = 2 \sum_{k = \max(\ca{n_{10}},\, \cb{n_{01}})}^{\cp{m}} \binom{\cp{m}}{k} 2^{-\cp{m}}, \qquad \cp{m} = \ca{n_{10}} + \cb{n_{01}}"
>

- $\ca{n_{10}}$ — questions **only A** got right (A's wins)
- $\cb{n_{01}}$ — questions **only B** got right (B's wins / regressions)
- $\cp{m} = \ca{n_{10}} + \cb{n_{01}}$ — total **disagreements** between the two models
- $\cc{p_{\text{McNemar}}}$ — exact probability that $\cp{m}$ fair coin flips land at least as lopsided as what you observed

</Eq>

Watch how dramatically the exact same 84% vs 78% headline changes depending on the disagreement structure underneath:

<PairedComparisonWidget />

Compare the two presets at 84% vs 78%:
- **Pure improvement ($n_{10} = 6, n_{01} = 0$):** Model A kept every single answer Model B got right and fixed 6 failures. Six heads in a row out of six fair coins happens only $2 \times (1/2)^6 = \mathbf{0.031}$ of the time! Unpaired said $p = 0.321$; paired reveals real evidence.
- **Trading wins ($n_{10} = 18, n_{01} = 12$):** Model A fixed 18 questions and *broke* 12 others. An 18–12 split out of 30 fair coins has $p = \mathbf{0.362}$ — pure churn.

The engineering rule is immediate: **always evaluate competing prompts or models on the exact same frozen questions, and always log $(n_{10}, n_{01})$ — never just two aggregate percentages.**

## The winner's curse: overfitting your eval set

At the end of the RAG lesson, we warned that tuning 125 retriever settings on 34 questions and reporting the best score is optimistic. Let's put an exact number on *how* optimistic.

<Predict

  options={[
    'About 80% — the true accuracy is 80%',
    'About 83% — a small bump from luck',
    'About 90% — a massive +10 percentage point mirage',
  ]}
  answer={2}
>

Each 50-question run has a standard error of $\sqrt{0.8 \times 0.2 / 50} = 5.7$ points. Draw 20 independent runs and pick the luckiest one, and the exact expected maximum is **89.9%** — a **+9.9 percentage point mirage**! In fact, **98.5%** of the time, the "winner" looks at least +5 points better than truth, even though it's worth zero on fresh data.

</Predict>

Because the maximum of $K$ independent draws is $\le k$ only if *all* $K$ draws are $\le k$, we get the exact distribution of **<Term id="eval-overfitting">eval overfitting</Term>** in one line:

<Eq
  words="\cc{P(\text{best of } \cb{K} \text{ prompts} \le k)} = \Big(\ca{P(\text{one prompt} \le k)}\Big)^{\cb{K}}"
  tex="\cc{F_{\max}(k)} = \big(\ca{F_{\text{Bin}(n, p)}(k)}\big)^{\cb{K}}"
>

- $\ca{F_{\text{Bin}(n, p)}(k)}$ — probability a **single** prompt scores at most $k$ right out of $n$ questions
- $\cb{K}$ — number of **candidate prompts or configs** tried on the same eval set
- $\cc{F_{\max}(k)}$ — exact cumulative probability for the **highest score** among all $K$ tries

</Eq>

Try different eval sizes $n$ and search counts $K$ below:

<EvalOverfitWidget />

Notice two things:
- On $n = 50$ at true accuracy 80%, even trying just **5** prompts creates a **+6.4 pp** average mirage (65.2% chance of ≥ +5 pp). Trying **20** creates **+9.9 pp** (98.5% chance of ≥ +5 pp).
- Raising $n$ to **500** shrinks the $K = 20$ mirage from +9.9 pp down to **+3.3 pp**, which is still larger than many real prompt improvements!

The cure is identical to <ModuleRef id="generalization" short />'s train/validation/test split: keep a **dev eval** you iterate on freely, and a **locked held-out test set** you touch only once before shipping a major change (and refresh periodically from fresh production logs).

## LLM-as-a-judge and position bias

Exact string matching works for multiple choice, tool-call arguments, or unit tests. It fails completely for open-ended prose ("was this refund refusal polite, accurate and concise?"). So teams increasingly use **<Term id="llm-as-a-judge">LLM-as-a-judge</Term>**: show a strong language model a question alongside Response A and Response B, and ask which is better.

It scales cheaply — and introduces systematic judge biases that don't average out as you add more questions:

- **Position bias:** many judges favour whichever response sits in Slot 1 (or Slot 2) whenever the pair is close.
- **Verbosity bias:** judges often prefer longer, more formatted answers even when shorter ones are more accurate.
- **Self-preference:** a model judging its own family's writing tends to score it higher than rival models of equal quality.

Explore position bias directly in the explicit probabilistic judge model below:

<JudgeBiasWidget />

Leave the true advantage at **0 pp** (two identical models) with a +20 pp Slot-1 bias:
- **A always first:** the judge reports A wins **65.0%** to **35.0%** — a completely fabricated 30-point landslide.
- **B always first:** the exact same judge flips to B winning **65.0%** to **35.0%**.
- **Position-swapped:** run every pair twice — `(A, B)` and `(B, A)` — and count a win only when both orderings agree. The bias cancels to **exact zero** (28.6% vs 28.6% for any bias $b$!), and the **42.8% flip rate** gives you a free alarm telling you how inconsistent your judge is.

And now set true advantage to **+10 pp** (A truly better: 35% vs 25%) and choose **B always first**: position bias *reverses the truth* and declares B the winner 60.5% to 39.5%! Position swapping recovers A's real lead cleanly (33.6% vs 26.6%).

### The hidden tax of a noisy binary grader

Even when a binary pass/fail LLM judge has *no* directional bias — say it simply misgrades each answer with probability $1 - q$ (accuracy $q$) — noise silently shrinks every true improvement you try to measure:

<Eq
  words="\cc{\text{observed accuracy gap}} = \big(2\cb{q} - 1\big) \times \ca{\text{true accuracy gap}}"
  tex="\cc{\Delta_{\text{obs}}} = (2\cb{q} - 1)\,\ca{\Delta}"
>

- $\ca{\Delta} = p_1 - p_0$ — the **true** accuracy improvement of the new system
- $\cb{q}$ — the grader's **accuracy** ($0.5$ = random coin, $1.0$ = exact oracle)
- $\cc{\Delta_{\text{obs}}}$ — the **attenuated gap** the noisy grader actually measures on average

</Eq>

At $q = 85\%$, $2q - 1 = 0.70$: a real +5.0% gain shrinks to **+3.5%** — and on a paired eval, independent grading errors also invent $2q(1 - q) \approx 25.5\%$ **fake disagreements** on questions where both models actually gave the same verdict! Does that mean LLM judges are useless? Not at all — because they cost a fraction of human grading, you can sometimes run 5× as many items and still come out ahead on cost. You'll discover the exact crossover in the lab.

<Misconception>

"An 85%-accurate LLM judge is good enough for any eval — 85% is pretty high!" It depends completely on the size of the gain you are trying to detect and how many questions you have. For a +5% lift with 2,000 cheap questions, an 85% judge works and saves money. For a subtle +3% lift — or a small 300-question benchmark — an 85% judge shrinks the signal to +2.1% and floods your McNemar test with fake disagreements, making it mathematically impossible to reach 85% detection power.

</Misconception>

## In code

Both exact McNemar p-values and the exact Winner's Curse expectation take twenty lines of standard-library Python:

```python
import math

def mcnemar_exact_p(n10, n01):
    """Exact two-sided McNemar p-value from discordant counts."""
    m = n10 + n01
    if m == 0 or n10 == n01:
        return 1.0
    hi = max(n10, n01)
    tail = sum(math.comb(m, k) for k in range(hi, m + 1)) * (0.5 ** m)
    return min(1.0, 2.0 * tail)

def winners_curse_expected_max(n, p, K):
    """Exact expected highest accuracy among K identical Binomial(n, p) prompts."""
    cdf, prev, exp_max = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0
    for k in range(n + 1):
        pk = math.comb(n, k) * (p ** k) * ((1 - p) ** (n - k))
        cdf = min(1.0, cdf + pk)
        pow_k = cdf ** K
        exp_max += (pow_k - prev) * (k / n)
        prev = pow_k
    return exp_max

print(f"McNemar (6 wins, 0 losses):   p = {mcnemar_exact_p(6, 0):.4f}")
print(f"McNemar (18 wins, 12 losses): p = {mcnemar_exact_p(18, 12):.4f}")
for K in (1, 5, 20):
    em = winners_curse_expected_max(50, 0.80, K)
    print(f"Best of {K:2d} identical prompts (n=50, true 80%): expected max = {100*em:.1f}% (+{100*max(0.0, em-0.80):.1f} pp)")
```

## Lab: Size Your Eval Suite

Every eval suite balances three numbers: **<Term id="statistical-power">statistical power</Term>** (probability you detect a real improvement when one exists), **false-positive rate** (probability you ship a no-op change by luck), and **grading cost**.

- **Brief 3 (High-Stakes +3% Lift: power ≥ 85%, strict FPR ≤ 2%):** Only 5 of 264 designs pass — **zero** unpaired designs and **zero** LLM-judge designs pass at any $n \le 2{,}000$! The cheapest costs **7,500 credits**: **Paired + Exact human/unit test at $n = 1{,}000$, cap 2%** (85.4% power, 1.47% FPR). When the effect is small and false alarms are expensive, only exact paired grading preserves enough signal-to-noise ratio.

Notice one pattern across all three briefs: **every single optimum is Paired.** Paying 1.5× per item to evaluate both models on the same frozen questions beats unpaired grading every time.

<Takeaways>

- **Always run paired evals** on the exact same frozen questions and track $(n_{10}, n_{01})$ disagreements with McNemar's test. Shared easy and hard questions cancel out, giving far sharper statistical power than two separate samples.
- **Beware the winner's curse:** choosing the best of $K = 20$ identical prompts on 50 questions creates a **+9.9 pp** mirage on average. Keep a dev eval for iteration and a locked held-out test set for release decisions.
- **Position bias doesn't average out with bigger $n$.** Always position-swap pairwise LLM judges `(A, B)` and `(B, A)`, count only consistent verdicts, and monitor the flip rate as a health check.
- **Noisy graders attenuate signal by $(2q - 1)$** and inject fake disagreements into paired tests. Cheap LLM judges win when questions are abundant and gains are ≥ 5%; exact deterministic tests or human grading become mandatory for small benchmarks or subtle ≤ 3% lifts.
- **Size your eval suite before you run experiments**, using power and false-positive targets — otherwise you can't tell whether a flat chart means your idea failed or your eval was too small to see it.

</Takeaways>

<InTheWild>

Mature AI engineering teams treat evals as a tiered test pyramid, just like software testing: (1) fast **deterministic assertions** (schema validity, regex/code checks, exact SQL or unit-test pass rates — $q = 1$, pennies to run in CI on every commit), (2) **paired LLM-as-a-judge** suites with position-swapping and calibration against a human-labelled golden set (measuring judge agreement $q$ and flip rate before trusting it), and (3) **periodic human review** plus online A/B metrics on real user traffic. Public leaderboards (LMSYS Chatbot Arena, SWE-bench, GPQA) wrestle with every phenomenon in this lesson: contamination and eval overfitting force labs to build fresh private held-out benchmarks (like SWE-bench Verified and LiveBench), and Chatbot Arena uses blind randomised slot ordering with Bradley–Terry / Elo ratings specifically to neutralise position bias.
